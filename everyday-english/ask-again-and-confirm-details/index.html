Listening repair · Hear → commit → verify

How to ask again and confirm names, numbers, and times in English

A practical hear–commit–verify guide for the moments when one missed letter, number, name, or time changes what you need to do next.

Don’t guess the missing detail.

Catch it, ask for it again, and confirm it before the conversation moves on.

Quick answer

When you miss one important detail, name the missing part and confirm what you think you heard: “Sorry, I missed the gate. Did you say D14?” If two values are possible, ask which one is right: “Was that 7:20 or 7:40?” After the answer, read it back once. This is clearer and more useful than pretending you understood or asking the speaker to repeat everything.

Use the smallest useful repair

A three-step response ladder

Move from a broad repeat to a precise confirmation, then close with one short read-back.

1

Repeat

You missed the whole detail.

“Sorry, I missed the gate. Could you say that again?”

Naming the missing part gives the speaker a smaller, easier request.

2

Confirm

You think you heard the detail.

“Did you say gate D14?”

The speaker can answer yes or correct one precise item.

3

Read back

The detail affects what you do next.

“So that’s D14, boarding at 7:20—right?”

One short read-back checks the two details as a usable instruction.

Choose by what you actually heard

What should you say next?

  1. You heard almost nothingAsk for one repeat“Sorry, could you say that again?”
  2. One part is missingName that part“Sorry, what was the gate number?”
  3. You have a likely answerConfirm the candidate“Did you say D14?”
  4. Two details guide the next actionRead both back once“D14 at 7:20—is that right?”

Common failure points

Repair the interaction, not your confidence

A useful response gives the other person a clear way to help.

Saying only “What?”

Name the missing item: “Sorry, what was the boarding time?”

Guessing and moving on

Offer your best candidate as a question: “Did you say D14?”

Repeating several possible answers as if all were correct

Ask the speaker to choose: “Was that 6:40 or 7:40?” Then keep only the confirmed value.

Apologizing for too long

Use one short apology, then make the request: “Sorry, could you repeat the time?”

Same move, new setting

Transfer the skill

Do not memorize one airport sentence. Reuse the confirmation pattern wherever a detail matters.

Coffee counter

The name or drink size was unclear.

“Sorry, was that Mina with a medium latte?”
Class or meeting

The room and start time came too quickly.

“Could I confirm that—room 214 at 3:30?”
Hospital hallway

A coworker gave a non-clinical meeting place and time.

“Sorry, did you say the west lobby at twelve-fifteen?”

Short answers

Questions learners ask

Is it rude to ask someone to repeat a number or time?

No. A short, specific request usually sounds attentive. Name the part you missed and avoid blaming the speaker.

Should I say “repeat” or “say that again”?

Both work. “Could you say that again?” is broadly natural; “Could you repeat the gate number?” is useful when you can name the exact detail.

What if I am not sure between two numbers?

Offer the two candidates as a question, listen to the correction, and then read back only the confirmed number.

Does replaying the practice audio count as failure?

No. The page records a replay as assisted practice so you can distinguish first-listen recall from successful repair. Both are useful skills.
