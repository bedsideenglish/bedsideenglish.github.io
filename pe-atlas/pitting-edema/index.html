Pitting edema grading

Tells pitting edema (fluid that can be pressed out of the tissue) from non-pitting swelling, and grades how deep and how slowly the dent recovers. It helps sort out heart, kidney, liver, venous and protein-loss causes of leg swelling.

How to do it

  1. Compare both legs with the patient lying down: note swelling, skin colour, warmth, and whether it is symmetric. Start at the dorsum of the feet and work up.
  2. Press firmly with your thumb over a bony surface (the medial tibia, the medial malleolus or the dorsum of the foot) and hold for at least 5 seconds.
  3. Release and look and feel for a dent. Note its depth and how long it takes to fill back in.
  4. Repeat at higher levels up the leg to find the upper limit of the swelling, and check the sacrum and the back in a bedbound patient.
  5. Grade the result and describe it with the level reached, for example 'pitting edema to the knees, 2+, symmetric'.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'm going to press on your shin to check for swelling. Tell me if it's tender.
  2. I'll hold for a few seconds. You'll feel firm pressure.
  3. Thank you. Does this leg feel heavier or more swollen than the other?

A positive finding

A dent that stays after release means pitting edema. Bilateral, symmetric pitting that rises with the day points to systemic causes (heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, venous insufficiency, drugs such as calcium-channel blockers). Unilateral swelling raises concern for deep vein thrombosis or local venous or lymphatic obstruction.

A negative finding

No dent means the swelling is not pitting: think of lymphedema, lipedema, myxedema or chronic venous skin change. Absence of pitting does not exclude early or mild fluid overload, since a small amount of excess fluid may not leave a visible pit.

Evidence & limitations

Grading is by the depth of the pit and how fast it rebounds, and it is only semi-quantitative: a commonly used scale is 1+ (about 2 mm, rebounds at once), 2+ (about 4 mm, under 15 seconds), 3+ (about 6 mm, 1 minute or more) and 4+ (about 8 mm, 2 to 5 minutes). Observers often disagree on the grade, and a pit is usually not visible until several litres of excess fluid have accumulated. Pitting is not specific for any one disease, so interpret it with the history, the jugular venous pressure, the heart and lungs, urinalysis and albumin.

Points to remember

  1. Press on a bony surface and hold at least 5 seconds. A quick poke on soft tissue under-calls the pit.
  2. Always compare both legs and note the upper level of the edema; the pattern (symmetric or not) matters more than the grade.
  3. In a bedbound patient fluid collects in the sacrum and flanks, so check the back, not only the ankles.
