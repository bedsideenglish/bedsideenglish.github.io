Finger-pulp capillary refill time

Assess peripheral perfusion by blanching a fingertip and timing colour return after release. This card uses the finger-pulp method.

See the examination

Inspect the baseline colour with the hand supported at heart level.

Blanch the finger pulp: hold pressure for 5 real seconds.

Release fully: start the clock now; pale colour is an example.

Stop at return to surrounding colour; record measured seconds.

How to do it

  1. Explain and obtain consent. Use good light, note ambient and hand temperature, and support the relaxed hand at heart level or just above.
  2. Inspect the baseline colour of the distal finger pulp. Apply enough pressure to blanch it and maintain that pressure for 5 seconds using a timer.
  3. Release completely. Start timing at release, stop when the compressed area returns to the surrounding skin colour, and record the actual seconds and site.
  4. Interpret alongside skin temperature, pulse, blood pressure, mental status and the clinical situation. If repeating, keep the method and conditions consistent.

What to say to the patient

  1. I will press the pad of your finger briefly, then watch how quickly the colour comes back. Is that okay?
  2. Keep your hand relaxed here while I support it.

A positive finding

Delayed return can indicate impaired peripheral perfusion, but cold-induced vasoconstriction and other factors also prolong it. Treat it as part of the circulation assessment, not as proof of shock or dehydration.

A negative finding

Prompt refill does not exclude shock or serious illness. A reassuring finger result must not override other abnormal findings.

Evidence & limitations

The Resuscitation Council UK finger method uses 5 seconds of pressure and describes refill as usually under 2 seconds. Adult volunteer data show dependence on age, sex and temperature; record a measured value rather than apply one universal cutoff.

Points to remember

  1. The colour panels illustrate blanching and return only. The playback is not a clock, a skin-colour reference or a calibrated perfusion model.
  2. Do not time from the beginning of compression or while still pressing. Report poor visual assessability rather than invent a precise result.
