Homans' sign

A traditional bedside screen for deep vein thrombosis (DVT) of the calf: calf pain on passive dorsiflexion of the foot. Know it because examiners and patients still mention it, but do not rely on it.

How to do it

  1. Patient lies supine with the leg relaxed and the knee straight (or slightly bent).
  2. Steady the knee with one hand.
  3. With the other hand on the sole, gently dorsiflex the foot (toes toward the shin).
  4. Ask about pain in the calf; compare with the other leg.
  5. Do it together with the rest of the leg exam: calf tenderness, swelling, pitting edema, and calf circumference on both sides.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'd like to examine your legs now. Is that all right?
  2. Please let your leg relax completely. I'll do the moving.
  3. I'm going to bend your foot up toward you. Tell me if you feel any pain in your calf.

A positive finding

Calf pain on dorsiflexion. Historically read as 'DVT more likely', but in studies a positive sign barely changes the probability: muscle strain, a ruptured Baker's cyst, cellulitis and other calf problems all hurt too.

A negative finding

No calf pain. This does NOT rule out DVT; many patients with a proven DVT have a negative sign.

Evidence & limitations

Likelihood ratios in published studies are close to 1 (reported sensitivity roughly 10–50%, specificity roughly 40–90%), so the sign is not diagnostically useful. Estimate pretest probability with the Wells score, then use D-dimer and/or compression ultrasonography.

Points to remember

  1. Be gentle: forceful dorsiflexion adds nothing, and there is a theoretical concern about dislodging a clot.
  2. Always examine and compare both legs.
  3. Say what you are checking and what you would do next: 'I'd calculate a Wells score and arrange a D-dimer or an ultrasound.'
