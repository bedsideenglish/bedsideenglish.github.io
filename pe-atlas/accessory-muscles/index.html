Work of breathing and tripod posture

Observe respiratory effort and the posture the patient chooses before disturbing them. Leaning forward with the arms braced can be a response to breathlessness; the posture does not identify its cause.

How to do it

  1. Introduce yourself and assess whether the patient can speak comfortably. Observe their preferred position without asking them to lie flat.
  2. If seated and leaning forward, note whether the hands or forearms are braced on the thighs or another stable surface.
  3. Observe the neck and chest during quiet breathing: accessory-muscle recruitment, intercostal/suprasternal recession, symmetry and respiratory pattern. Measure respiratory rate and check oxygen saturation when indicated.
  4. Assess speech, alertness and overall distress. Marked effort, inability to speak, stridor, cyanosis or exhaustion warrants prompt clinical help; do not delay for a complete teaching examination.
  5. Let the patient remain supported and comfortable. Combine inspection with the relevant cardiopulmonary examination and reassess changes.

What to say to the patient

  1. Stay in the position that makes breathing easiest. You don't need to lie down.
  2. I'll watch your breathing first. You can rest your arms on your thighs.
  3. If speaking is difficult, take your time; I will get help.

A positive finding

Bracing, visible accessory-muscle recruitment or recession suggests increased work of breathing. Judge urgency from the whole presentation; this is not specific for asthma, COPD or any single diagnosis.

A negative finding

No visible accessory effort or tripod posture does not exclude respiratory disease or hypoxaemia. A tiring patient may show less effort despite deterioration.

Evidence & limitations

This is an observational assessment of posture and effort. Respiratory rate, oxygenation and response to intervention must be assessed in the real patient; the animation has no calibrated breathing rate.

Points to remember

  1. Do not ask a breathless patient to repeatedly reproduce distress or force a different posture for the demonstration.
  2. The mannequin shows a supported forward-leaning posture and a small chest expansion only. It does not simulate SCM/scalene contraction, recession, cyanosis, breath sounds or the severity of illness.
