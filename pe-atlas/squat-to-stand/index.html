Squat-to-stand

Squatting increases venous return and afterload, which enlarges the left ventricle; standing quickly does the opposite. Murmurs of HCM and MVP get softer on squatting and louder on standing, the reverse of most other murmurs. Together with Valsalva it is the standard bedside test of dynamic obstruction.

How to do it

  1. With the patient standing (if safe), find the murmur and note its loudness at the left sternal border or apex.
  2. Ask the patient to squat fully while you keep the stethoscope on the same spot (or re-place it quickly). Listen for about 10 to 15 seconds.
  3. Have the patient stand up quickly and listen to the first 10 to 15 seconds after standing.
  4. Compare the murmur in squatting, in standing, and at rest.
  5. If the patient cannot squat, raise both legs passively while supine for the squatting-like effect, and stand them up for the opposite.

What to say to the patient

  1. Could you squat down all the way for me? I'll listen the whole time.
  2. Good. Now stand up quickly.
  3. Thank you. Take your time. Let me know if you feel dizzy.

A positive finding

Murmur SOFTER on squatting and LOUDER on standing: HCM (dynamic LV outflow obstruction). In MVP, the click-murmur comes earlier and lasts longer on standing and later on squatting. Aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation and most other murmurs go the opposite way (louder on squatting).

A negative finding

No clear change: not specific. A fixed obstruction such as aortic stenosis usually gets softer on standing and louder on squatting, so the direction of change, not just its presence, is the finding.

Evidence & limitations

In Lembo et al., the change of the murmur with squatting and standing helped separate HCM from aortic stenosis. Reported sensitivities and specificities vary widely with study, patient group and examiner, so cite them as ranges; echocardiography is the reference standard.

Points to remember

  1. Dizziness or a vasovagal faint can follow a rapid stand. Stay close and have a chair nearby.
  2. Weigh the risk before asking a patient with severe aortic stenosis or exertional syncope to squat and stand.
  3. Squatting for many seconds is tiring; listen quickly and keep the order consistent (rest, squat, stand).
