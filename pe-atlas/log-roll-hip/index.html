Hip log roll — passive rotation and pain

Gently rotate the relaxed, extended leg as a unit to see whether hip rotation reproduces familiar pain. This card examines pain with passive rotation, rather than the separate dial-test interpretation of capsular laxity.

How to do it

  1. Ask the patient to point to the usual pain and obtain consent. Do not perform a provocative roll when fracture, dislocation or another acute injury requires protection and assessment first.
  2. Position the patient supine with the legs extended and relaxed. Note the resting rotation and compare the less painful side first when feasible.
  3. Place one hand on the distal thigh above the knee and the other on the distal leg. Guide the whole limb together; do not use the foot as a lever to twist the knee.
  4. Keeping the hip near neutral flexion and the pelvis still, gently rotate the thigh inward and then outward through a small, comfortable arc. The patella and foot turn together; do not substitute abduction of the leg or rolling of the trunk.
  5. Ask whether any pain matches the usual symptom and where it is felt: groin, lateral hip, back or elsewhere. Stop for pain or resistance; compare the range and symptom response with the other side.
  6. Return the leg to a comfortable resting position. Record pain location, familiar-symptom reproduction, asymmetry and any limitation; use the rest of the hip and lumbar examination to interpret it.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'll gently roll your relaxed leg inwards and outwards. Please let me do the moving.
  2. Does that bring on the pain you came in with? Please point to where you feel it.
  3. Tell me if it becomes uncomfortable; I will stop.

A positive finding

Reproduction of familiar deep groin/hip pain raises suspicion that the hip joint contributes to symptoms. A painful or asymmetric roll is not a diagnosis of a labral tear, osteoarthritis or instability. Record other pain locations without automatically calling them intra-articular disease.

A negative finding

A painless roll does not exclude hip disease. A small comfortable arc is only one part of the examination, and bilateral disease can make comparison with the opposite side misleading.

Evidence & limitations

Clinical examination reports describe log roll as an aid to recognising a symptomatic hip. Published protocols and definitions of a positive result vary; this card does not claim a universal likelihood ratio or a cutoff angle. Interpret the finding with history, other examination findings and indicated investigations.

Points to remember

  1. This is passive hip rotation with the knee extended, not FADIR with the hip and knee flexed. Keep the knee and ankle relaxed, without torsional stress.
  2. Increased external rotation alone may have several explanations. Capsular laxity/dial testing is a different interpretation and is not established by this pain-provocation card.
  3. The right leg and guiding hands are shown; repeat on the left in practice. The displayed angles and motion arcs teach direction, not normal ranges or a measured result. The picture does not show cartilage or a labral lesion.
