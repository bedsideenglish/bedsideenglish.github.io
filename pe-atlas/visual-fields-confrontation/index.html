Confrontation visual fields: each eye, four quadrants

Screen monocular peripheral visual fields against the examiner’s field. The oval is the PATIENT’S field view, not a retinal map; the arrow shows a target entering a quadrant.

How to do it

  1. Sit facing the patient at the same eye level, about an arm’s length apart. Explain that central fixation must be maintained.
  2. To test the patient’s right eye, cover the patient’s left eye without pressure; close your right eye and ask the patient to fixate on your open left eye. Reverse for the other eye.
  3. Place a small moving finger or target midway between your faces. Bring it from beyond peripheral vision inward in each quadrant; ask when it first appears and compare with your own detection.
  4. Test upper/lower temporal and upper/lower nasal quadrants separately, with unpredictable timing. Repeat any uncertain area while checking fixation.
  5. Record the eye and field area involved. Obtain formal perimetry for suspected defects; assess binocular extinction separately if relevant.

What to say to the patient

  1. Keep looking at my open eye. Do not look toward my moving finger.
  2. Say “now” as soon as you first see the target.

A positive finding

Repeated failure or delayed detection in a consistent field region suggests a defect. Distribution across BOTH eyes helps localisation; a single missed target is not a mapped hemianopia.

A negative finding

No gross asymmetry was detected by this screening method. Small scotomas and early glaucoma can be missed; persistent symptoms still need formal assessment.

Evidence & limitations

Confrontation is a coarse comparison, not threshold perimetry. The drawn oval and arrows carry no angular scale and do not depict a normal field boundary or a patient’s loss.

Points to remember

  1. The examiner needs an adequate reference field; otherwise the comparison is unreliable.
  2. Do not press on the covered globe or let the target drift close to only one face.
