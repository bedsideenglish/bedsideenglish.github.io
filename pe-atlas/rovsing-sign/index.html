Rovsing's sign

An indirect sign of appendicitis. Pressing in the left lower quadrant pushes gas and bowel contents toward the right, and the stretched inflamed area in the right lower quadrant hurts, even though the pressure was applied on the opposite side.

How to do it

  1. Patient lies supine with the knees slightly bent to relax the abdominal wall. Ask where the pain is and examine that area last.
  2. Place the flat of your right hand over the left lower quadrant, in the mirror position of McBurney's point.
  3. Press in deeply and steadily. Some examiners also slide the hand up the descending colon toward the left costal margin.
  4. Ask whether pain appears in the right lower quadrant, not under your hand. Watch the face.
  5. Release and ask again. Compare with McBurney's point tenderness and the other signs.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'm going to press on the left side of your belly. Please tell me if you feel any pain, and where.
  2. Does it hurt on the right side when I press on the left?
  3. I'm letting go now. Is the pain better or worse?

A positive finding

Pain in the right lower quadrant when the left lower quadrant is pressed. Appendicitis with local peritoneal irritation becomes more likely. It does not localise the cause by itself: other causes of right-sided peritonitis can give the same result.

A negative finding

No right-sided pain. Appendicitis is NOT excluded; the sign is absent in many patients with appendicitis.

Evidence & limitations

Evidence is limited and studies are small and of low quality. Reported sensitivity is roughly 30 to 70% and specificity roughly 60 to 90%, with a positive LR of about 2 to 3 in the pooled data summarised by McGee and Wagner; it adds only moderate weight when positive and little when negative. It is best read together with migration of pain, RLQ tenderness, rebound and labs or imaging.

Points to remember

  1. Press away from the pain first; if the left side hurts, the sign cannot be interpreted.
  2. A generally tender or guarded abdomen can give a false positive; ask where the pain is felt.
  3. Do not rely on it to exclude appendicitis; the diagnosis rests on the whole picture and imaging.
