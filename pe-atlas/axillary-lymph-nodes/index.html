Axillary lymph nodes · supported arm and walls

Systematically palpate an indicated axilla with a relaxed, supported arm. The enlarged map distinguishes walls and depth; node symbols represent search regions, not enlarged nodes in a patient.

See the examination

A · Right axilla: relax the shoulder and support the forearm. Contact fingers approach gently; no node pathology is drawn.

B · View from below: central region between chest wall and upper arm. Symbols show search regions.

C · Anterior/pectoral fold, at the front of the axillary hollow.

D · Posterior/subscapular fold, at the back; do not confuse it with the anterior fold.

E · Lateral/humeral region along the medial upper arm.

F · Separate depth schematic: assess high toward the apex only as accessible and tolerated.

How to do it

  1. Explain the indication and examination, obtain consent and offer a chaperone. Provide privacy, limited exposure and the right to stop. Ask about pain and recent skin infection, vaccination or surgery.
  2. Seat the patient comfortably. For the right axilla, support their relaxed right forearm with your right hand and examine with your left. Allow only the abduction needed for access; do not make the patient hold the arm rigidly overhead.
  3. Gently place finger pads high into the axilla and bring them down against the chest wall to assess the central region. Use controlled, overlapping palpation rather than pinching or digging.
  4. Assess the anterior (pectoral) fold, posterior (subscapular) fold and medial upper-arm/lateral (humeral) region. Assess the high/apical region gently as accessible; these are three-dimensional groups, not dots on a flat chest.
  5. Compare the other side. Describe palpable nodes by site, number, actual size, tenderness, texture, mobility and skin relation; investigate a persistent or concerning finding in context.

What to say to the patient

  1. I will support your arm and gently feel the armpit. Keep it relaxed, and tell me if you want me to stop.

A positive finding

An enlarged, hard, fixed or otherwise concerning node requires further assessment. Tender or mobile nodes can be reactive, but those features do not prove a benign cause.

A negative finding

No palpable nodes does not exclude breast malignancy, lymphoma or microscopic nodal involvement. Palpation does not replace indicated imaging or tissue diagnosis.

Evidence & limitations

The wall map is a view from below into the right axilla. The apical panel is a separate depth schematic, not a projection of all groups onto the skin or a cancer-stage map.

Points to remember

  1. Assess supraclavicular or inguinal nodes separately when indicated. This card does not teach a complete breast, groin or whole-body lymph-node examination.
