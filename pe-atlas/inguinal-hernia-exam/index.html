Groin hernia examination — surface assessment

Locate a groin swelling in relation to the inguinal ligament and pubic tubercle, with standing observation and a cough impulse.

How to do it

  1. Explain the intimate examination, ask consent, offer a chaperone and arrange privacy/draping. Ask the patient to indicate any swelling and pain.
  2. If safe, inspect standing at rest and during one cough or tolerated strain. Compare both groins; inspect relevant scars and any scrotal extension when indicated and consented.
  3. Identify ASIS, inguinal ligament and pubic tubercle. Gently assess the swelling and cough impulse; describe its relationship to these landmarks.
  4. Reassess supine if appropriate. A tender irreducible swelling, vomiting or obstruction symptoms needs urgent assessment; do not force reduction.
  5. Record side, location, tenderness and reducibility. Use imaging or surgical assessment when uncertain or an occult/femoral hernia remains suspected.

What to say to the patient

  1. May I examine your groin with a chaperone present? Please cough once; tell me if it hurts.

A positive finding

An expansile cough impulse supports a hernia. Inguinal swellings are typically above the ligament and superomedial to the pubic tubercle; femoral swellings lie below and inferolateral. Large masses can obscure these relations.

A negative finding

No visible swelling or cough impulse does not exclude an occult or incarcerated hernia; a painful mass may have no impulse.

Evidence & limitations

Surface examination locates the swelling but does not reliably distinguish direct from indirect inguinal hernias. The drawn lump sites are categorical examples.

Points to remember

  1. Do not infer direct versus indirect from which side of the finger feels the impulse.
  2. This card omits scrotal invagination, internal ring probing and genital detail; those require separate supervised instruction when indicated.
