Ober test: supported hip adduction

Assess passive hip adduction with the pelvis stabilised in side lying.

See the examination

Left side down; support the upper leg with knee bent 90°

Gently extend the abducted hip; pelvis stays stacked

Allow supported adduction; do not press down

Lift the supported leg back into abduction

Return the hip to neutral; compare the other side

Drag to rotate freely

How to do it

  1. Lie on the non-test side with the lower hip and knee flexed. Keep shoulders and pelvis stacked; test the upper leg.
  2. Stabilise the upper pelvis. Support the upper thigh/knee, flex that knee about 90 degrees, and gently abduct then extend the hip.
  3. Maintain the hip alignment and let the thigh descend into adduction under gravity while controlling its weight. Stop at pain, resistance or pelvic movement.
  4. Record the available motion and symptoms, then support the leg back to the starting position. Compare sides using the same variant.

What to say to the patient

  1. Let this leg relax. I will support it as it comes down; tell me if it hurts.

A positive finding

Restricted adduction with the pelvis held still describes a mobility finding; it does not by itself establish iliotibial-band tightness or ITB syndrome.

A negative finding

Comfortable adduction does not exclude a source of lateral hip or knee pain.

Evidence & limitations

Cadaveric work challenges a simple ITB-length interpretation. The knee-flexed and knee-straight variants are not interchangeable.

Points to remember

  1. Pelvic rolling or hip flexion can create apparent extra adduction. Do not force the leg to reach the couch.
  2. The animation stops at illustrative neutral adduction. It does not depict a measured endpoint, tissue tension or a positive result.
