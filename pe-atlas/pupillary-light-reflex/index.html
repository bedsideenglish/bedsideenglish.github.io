Pupillary light reflex and swinging-light test

Assess direct/consensual light responses, then compare afferent input between eyes. A relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) is a response asymmetry, not a difference in resting pupil size.

How to do it

  1. Explain the bright light. Dim the room and ask the patient to fixate on a distant target. Record pupil size, shape and equality before illumination.
  2. Illuminate the right eye from below/aside; observe the right direct and left consensual constriction. Repeat on the left.
  3. For the swinging-light test, use equal brightness, distance and exposure, about 3 seconds per eye. Transfer the whole light quickly between eyes and repeat.
  4. Watch both pupils as light moves from one eye to the other. Relative enlargement when illuminating one eye identifies weaker afferent input from that eye, if the efferent response permits assessment.
  5. Document which eye has the RAPD, together with acuity, fields and pupil findings; investigate the clinical cause. The last two panels are a separate LEFT-RAPD example.

What to say to the patient

  1. Please keep looking at that distant point, even when I shine the light.
  2. The light will be bright. Please tell me if you need a break.

A positive finding

On transfer from the better eye to the affected eye, both pupils constrict less or enlarge relative to their previous size. This supports asymmetric retinal/optic-nerve dysfunction; interpret with the rest of the visual examination.

A negative finding

Equal sustained responses give no demonstrable RAPD. Symmetric bilateral afferent disease can still be severe; this is not a test that excludes all visual-pathway disease.

Evidence & limitations

The illustrated 3-second comparison follows a published clinical method. Pupil radii and panel times are explanatory, not measured diameters, clinical response latency or RAPD grades.

Points to remember

  1. Near fixation can cause constriction and obscure the comparison.
  2. Anisocoria, pharmacological dilation and an efferent defect require separate interpretation. If one pupil is fixed, assessment may still be possible by watching the reactive pupil.
