Anterior drawer of the ankle: ATFL assessment

Assess anterior talar laxity relative to the stabilised lower leg.

Cup the heel while stabilising the distal tibia

Draw the heel forwards relative to the tibia

How to do it

  1. After assessing for fracture or major acute injury, lie the patient supine. Support the leg with the knee flexed to relax the calf; the figure shows this supported supine variant.
  2. Relax the ankle in slight plantarflexion, about 10–20 degrees. Stabilise the distal tibia with one hand and cup the calcaneus with the other.
  3. Gently draw the heel/talus anteriorly relative to the tibia without substituting plantarflexion, inversion or whole-leg movement.
  4. Compare translation and endpoint with the other side, noting pain and guarding. Release the force before returning to rest.

What to say to the patient

  1. Let your foot go loose. I will hold your shin and gently bring your heel forward. Tell me if it hurts.

A positive finding

Greater anterior translation or a softer endpoint than the other ankle supports lateral ligament insufficiency, especially ATFL involvement.

A negative finding

A firm or symmetric examination does not exclude a partial injury; acute pain and guarding can mask laxity.

Evidence & limitations

Technique and normal laxity vary. This bedside comparison does not apply a universal millimetre cutoff or establish injury grade.

Points to remember

  1. A pain-limited test is inconclusive; do not repeatedly force an acutely injured ankle.
  2. The arrow specifies anterior force. The rig has no independent talar translation: no displacement is animated and ankle bending is not used as a substitute. Heel contact is schematic.
