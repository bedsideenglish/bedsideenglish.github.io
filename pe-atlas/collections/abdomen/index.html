Abdomen & renal

21 techniques. Locate surface landmarks and follow abdominal examination techniques.

  • Murphy's signLooks for an inflamed gallbladder (acute cholecystitis): on a deep breath the gallbladder descends onto the examiner's fingers, and the pain makes the patient stop breathing in.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Psoas signLooks for an inflamed structure lying on the iliopsoas muscle. A retrocecal appendix touching the right psoas hurts when the muscle is stretched by extending the hip, or when it contracts against resistance.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Obturator signLooks for inflammation next to the obturator internus, typically a pelvic appendix or a pelvic abscess. Passive internal rotation of the flexed hip stretches the muscle and reproduces the pain.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Shifting dullnessDetects free fluid (ascites) in the abdomen. Fluid sinks to the dependent flank when the patient rolls, so a dull note over the flank becomes resonant on the side that is now uppermost, and dull on the side now below.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Liver edge palpation with inspirationLooks for an enlarged or firm liver. The liver edge moves down on a deep breath and meets the fingertips pressed under the right costal margin, so you can feel its edge, surface and consistency and measure how far it extends below the ribs.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Spleen palpation (right lateral decubitus)Looks for an enlarged spleen. With the patient rolled onto the right side the spleen falls forward and down, so an enlarged tip can meet the examiner's fingertips under the left costal margin during a deep breath in.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Rebound tenderness (Blumberg's sign)Looks for peritoneal irritation. Pressing in slowly and then letting go suddenly makes the inflamed peritoneum snap back, and the patient feels a sharp pain on release that is worse than the pain on pressing.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Rovsing's signAn indirect sign of appendicitis. Pressing in the left lower quadrant pushes gas and bowel contents toward the right, and the stretched inflamed area in the right lower quadrant hurts, even though the pressure was applied on the opposite side.Abdomen & renal↗
  • McBurney's point tendernessTests for localised tenderness over the usual position of the appendix. McBurney's point lies about one third of the way along the line from the right anterior superior iliac spine to the umbilicus.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Light then deep palpation of the abdomenMaps tenderness, guarding and masses across the nine regions of the abdomen. Light palpation finds superficial tenderness and voluntary or involuntary guarding; deep palpation finds organ edges, masses and deeper tenderness.Abdomen & renal↗
  • CVA tenderness (fist percussion)Looks for inflammation or stretch of the kidney capsule. A gentle fist strike over the costovertebral angle (CVA, below the 12th rib beside the spine) hurts when the kidney is inflamed, as in pyelonephritis, or distended by an obstructing stone.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Abdominal inspectionInspect abdominal contour and movement before touching the abdomen.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Abdominal auscultation: bowel soundsListen for bowel sounds before percussion or palpation.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Abdominal percussion: tympany and dullnessMap the percussion note across the abdomen after auscultation.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Liver span by percussionEstimate the vertical extent of hepatic dullness on the right midclavicular line. Find two sound transitions; a low palpable edge alone does not establish enlargement.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Castell's splenic percussion signScreen for splenic enlargement by comparing the percussion note at one left lower-chest interspace during expiration and inspiration. This card teaches Castell's sign; it does not map Traube's space.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Carnett's signCompare tenderness at one focal abdominal point with the wall relaxed and then tensed. This helps assess an abdominal-wall source of pain, especially in a chronic focal presentation.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Fluid wave (fluid thrill) for ascitesLook for an impulse transmitted between the flanks while dampening transmission through abdominal skin and fat. It is a supplementary sign of ascites, especially when fluid volume is large.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Abdominal inspection — low viewpoint and tangential lightSupplement the general abdominal inspection with a low line of sight and oblique light to appreciate surface contour.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Peritoneal irritation · cough and gentle percussionAssess pain provoked by small abdominal movement while minimising repeated painful examination; record the location reported by the patient.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Suprapubic bladder · gentle palpation and percussionRecognize suprapubic fullness and tenderness and understand when objective bladder assessment is needed.Abdomen & renal↗
