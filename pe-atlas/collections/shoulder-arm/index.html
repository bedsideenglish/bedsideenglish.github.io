Shoulder & arm

19 techniques. Explore movement, strength and focused upper-limb maneuvers.

  • Painful arc (60-120 degrees)Looks for subacromial impingement. As the arm is raised sideways, the rotator cuff tendons and bursa are squeezed under the acromion in the middle of the range, so pain appears between about 60 and 120 degrees and eases above that.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Empty can test (Jobe)Tests the supraspinatus. With the arm raised in the scapular plane and the thumb pointing down, the supraspinatus is the main muscle holding the arm up against a downward push. Weakness suggests a tear or tendon disease; pain without weakness suggests tendinopathy.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Drop arm testLooks for a large or full-thickness rotator cuff tear, mainly the supraspinatus. A shoulder with an intact cuff can hold the arm and lower it smoothly from 90 degrees of abduction; a torn one cannot.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Neer impingement testLooks for subacromial impingement. Raising the arm forward fully, with the arm turned inward and the scapula held still, pushes the greater tuberosity and the supraspinatus tendon against the front of the acromion and reproduces the pain.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Hawkins–Kennedy testScreens for subacromial impingement. With the arm raised to 90 degrees and the elbow bent, passive internal rotation pushes the greater tuberosity and the rotator cuff tendons (mainly supraspinatus) toward the coracoacromial arch and reproduces the pain.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Resisted shoulder external rotationAssess external-rotator function, mainly infraspinatus and teres minor, with the upper arm at the side. Separate pain-limited effort from reproducible weakness.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Cross-body adduction (AC joint)Provoke and localise shoulder pain while gently bringing the elevated arm across the chest. Pain precisely over the acromioclavicular (AC) joint is the finding of interest.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Elbow inspection and active range of motionDistinguish a change around the elbow joint from superficial olecranon swelling and document movement. Inspect, feel, then ask the patient to move; this is not a fracture-exclusion rule.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Shoulder apprehension and relocationAssess symptomatic anterior shoulder instability by asking about fear of the shoulder slipping during abduction/external rotation, then whether posterior support relieves that fear.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Phalen test: unforced wrist-flexion variantTry to reproduce the patient’s usual median-distribution symptoms during sustained wrist flexion.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Tinel sign at the carpal tunnelUse gentle percussion at the volar wrist to ask whether tingling radiates into median-innervated digits.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Speed test: resisted shoulder flexionProvoke familiar anterior shoulder pain while loading the long head of biceps with a supinated forearm.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Yergason test: resisted forearm supinationAssess familiar bicipital-groove pain or palpable snapping during resisted supination.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Resisted wrist extension (Cozen-type test)Reproduce familiar lateral elbow pain by loading the wrist extensors.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Resisted wrist flexion for medial elbow painLoad the common flexor origin while checking the location of familiar pain.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Finkelstein test — open-finger variantAssess familiar pain over the first dorsal wrist compartment (APL/EPB) using a gentle, explicitly defined Finkelstein variant.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Froment's sign — lateral paper pinchLook for thumb IP flexion used to compensate for weak ulnar-innervated adductor pollicis during a lateral pinch.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Modified Allen test — ulnar releaseDemonstrate a traditional bedside assessment of hand reperfusion after releasing the ulnar artery while radial compression continues; interpret with its limited predictive value.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Belly-press test — subscapularisAssess active shoulder internal rotation through the belly-press variant. This card does not demonstrate the behind-the-back lift-off test.Shoulder & arm↗
