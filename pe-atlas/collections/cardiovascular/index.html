Cardiovascular

15 techniques. Explore heart sounds, venous pressure and peripheral circulation.

  • Jugular venous pressure (JVP) at 45°Estimates central venous pressure (right atrial pressure) from the height of the pulsating column of blood in the internal jugular vein. It is the bedside sign of volume overload and right-sided heart failure.Cardiovascular↗
  • Hepatojugular (abdominojugular) refluxTests whether the right heart can handle a sudden extra load of venous return. Pressure on the right upper abdomen pushes blood toward the heart; a failing or stiff right heart cannot take it, and the JVP rises and stays up.Cardiovascular↗
  • Cardiac auscultation areas (A, P, T, M)Listens to the heart sounds and murmurs at four standard sites, each named for the valve whose sound is heard best there. Murmurs often radiate beyond their area, so the area is a guide to where to listen, not proof of which valve is diseased.Cardiovascular↗
  • Left lateral decubitus auscultationRolling the patient onto the left side brings the left ventricular apex against the chest wall, so low-pitched sounds from the left side of the heart become easier to hear: the mitral stenosis rumble, an opening snap, an S3 or S4 gallop.Cardiovascular↗
  • Sitting forward auscultationLeaning forward in held expiration brings the base of the heart and the aortic root closer to the chest wall. It is the position for the soft, high-pitched early diastolic murmur of aortic regurgitation and for a pericardial friction rub.Cardiovascular↗
  • Valsalva maneuver and murmursThe strain phase of the Valsalva maneuver reduces venous return and so lowers left ventricular volume. Most murmurs get softer, but the murmur of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) and the click-murmur of mitral valve prolapse (MVP) get louder or start earlier. It helps separate HCM from aortic stenosis at the bedside.Cardiovascular↗
  • Squat-to-standSquatting increases venous return and afterload, which enlarges the left ventricle; standing quickly does the opposite. Murmurs of HCM and MVP get softer on squatting and louder on standing, the reverse of most other murmurs. Together with Valsalva it is the standard bedside test of dynamic obstruction.Cardiovascular↗
  • Lower-limb pulses (femoral, popliteal, foot)Checks the arterial supply to the legs. Compare both sides at each level: femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis (DP) and posterior tibial (PT). A pulse that is weak or missing from a level down points to arterial narrowing or occlusion at or above that level.Cardiovascular↗
  • Pitting edema gradingTells pitting edema (fluid that can be pressed out of the tissue) from non-pitting swelling, and grades how deep and how slowly the dent recovers. It helps sort out heart, kidney, liver, venous and protein-loss causes of leg swelling.Cardiovascular↗
  • Precordial palpation: apex, heaves and thrillsLocate the apex impulse and feel for abnormal precordial movement.Cardiovascular↗
  • Radial, brachial and carotid pulse sitesLocate upper-limb arterial pulses and examine each carotid separately, distinguishing the pulse from adjacent tendons and muscles.Cardiovascular↗
  • Radio–femoral pulse delayCompare the onset of the right radial and a femoral pulse simultaneously to screen for aortic obstruction.Cardiovascular↗
  • Resting ankle–brachial index (Doppler)Compare each leg’s highest ankle systolic pressure with the higher brachial systolic pressure. Obtain separate right and left ABIs; a pulse palpation alone is not an ABI measurement.Cardiovascular↗
  • Sustained handgrip and murmur responseCompare a murmur during resting, tolerable isometric effort and recovery as one part of a complete cardiac examination.Cardiovascular↗
  • Buerger postural colour testObserve elevation pallor and dependent rubor as adjuncts to lower-limb arterial assessment, not a test for Buerger disease specifically.Cardiovascular↗
