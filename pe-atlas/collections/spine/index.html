Spine

8 techniques. Explore spinal examination and nerve tension maneuvers.

  • Straight leg raise (with Bragard's sign)Looks for tension on a lumbosacral nerve root, usually an L5 or S1 root irritated by a herniated disc. Raising the straight leg stretches the sciatic nerve; dorsiflexing the ankle adds tension (Bragard's sign) and helps separate nerve pain from tight hamstrings.Spine↗
  • Spurling's testLooks for cervical nerve root compression (cervical radiculopathy). Extending the neck and tilting and turning the head toward the symptomatic side narrows the intervertebral foramen on that side, and a gentle downward press can reproduce arm pain.Spine↗
  • Crossed straight leg raiseLook for familiar radicular pain in the symptomatic leg when the opposite leg is raised.Spine↗
  • Femoral nerve stretch — prone knee bendLook for reproduction of familiar anterior-thigh radicular pain by gently bending the knee in prone. This mainly assesses the upper lumbar/femoral nerve pathway, commonly L2–L4; it does not by itself locate a lesion.Spine↗
  • Spinal midline and paraspinal tendernessDistinguish focal midline bony tenderness from paraspinal muscle tenderness during a back examination. Gentle percussion is optional and guided by the clinical context.Spine↗
  • Adams forward bend: positioning and inspectionLook for trunk rotational prominence during a controlled forward bend.Spine↗
  • Modified Schober test — 10 cm above / 5 cm belowMeasure change in lumbar skin-mark separation during forward flexion, using the explicitly defined 15-cm modified Schober method.Spine↗
  • Seated slump — sequential loading and neck releaseAssess familiar radiating symptoms and their modulation by changing neural loading; distinguish these from normal stretch responses.Spine↗
