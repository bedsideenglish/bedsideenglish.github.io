Cross-body adduction (AC joint)

Provoke and localise shoulder pain while gently bringing the elevated arm across the chest. Pain precisely over the acromioclavicular (AC) joint is the finding of interest.

How to do it

  1. Explain and obtain consent. Ask the patient to point to the usual pain; inspect and palpate the AC region before provoking it.
  2. Seat the patient and gently raise the shoulder forward to about 90 degrees. Bend the elbow for comfort.
  3. Steady the shoulder girdle with one hand. Support the distal upper arm near the elbow with the other.
  4. Passively guide the arm horizontally across the chest toward the opposite shoulder. Keep the trunk facing forward; stop at pain or resistance.
  5. Ask the patient to point to the reproduced pain. Return the supported arm before releasing; compare the other side if appropriate.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'll support your arm and gently bring it across your chest.
  2. Keep facing forward. Tell me where it hurts, and I will stop.

A positive finding

Familiar focal pain at the AC joint supports an AC source in context. Posterior shoulder stretching or diffuse pain is not the same finding.

A negative finding

No focal AC pain does not exclude AC pathology or another shoulder problem. Interpret alongside the pain history, local tenderness and other examination findings.

Evidence & limitations

In a primary-care study of non-traumatic AC pain, traditional AC tests did not reliably discriminate an injection-confirmed AC pain source. Do not make a stand-alone diagnosis from this maneuver.

Points to remember

  1. Do not force a recently injured or visibly deformed shoulder. Avoid rotating the trunk to manufacture range.
  2. The surface marker indicates an approximate AC region on the stylised shoulder, not an exposed joint surface. The illustrated endpoint is not a required angle.
