Chest expansion (posterior, thumbs)

Compares how much the two sides of the lower chest move during a deep breath. Reduced or delayed movement on one side points to disease of that lung or pleura; reduced movement on both sides points to restrictive or hyperinflated chest disease.

See the examination

Hands around the lower ribs at the 10th rib level, thumbs 2–3 cm apart near the spine; breathing out

Deep breath in: both thumbs should move apart equally and at the same time

How to do it

  1. Sit the patient upright with the back exposed. Stand behind them.
  2. Place your hands flat on the lower back at about the level of the 10th rib, fingers wrapped around the sides of the chest and thumbs pointing toward the spine.
  3. Slide your hands inward so a fold of skin lifts between your thumbs and the midline, and your thumbs are about 2 to 3 cm from each other.
  4. Ask for a slow, deep breath in and watch how far each thumb moves away from the midline.
  5. Compare the two sides: are they equal, and do they start together? Repeat the same test at the upper chest if needed.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'd like to feel how your chest moves when you breathe. Could you sit up and lift your shirt at the back?
  2. I'm going to rest my hands on your back. Breathe in deeply through your mouth... and out.
  3. Thank you. Any pain when you breathe in deeply?

A positive finding

Reduced or lagging expansion on one side suggests lobar collapse, large pleural effusion, pneumothorax, consolidation or pleuritic pain on that side. Symmetric reduction suggests pulmonary fibrosis, COPD with hyperinflation, ankylosing spondylitis, or neuromuscular weakness.

A negative finding

Equal, symmetric movement makes a large one-sided lung or pleural process less likely. It does not exclude small effusions, mild bilateral disease or early pneumonia.

Evidence & limitations

Bedside assessment of chest expansion has limited accuracy and low observer agreement, so it is a supporting sign and not a stand-alone test. Reduced unilateral expansion is more useful when it fits with dull percussion, reduced breath sounds and reduced tactile fremitus; confirm with chest radiography or ultrasonography.

Points to remember

  1. Keep your thumbs relaxed and light; pressing in hides a small movement.
  2. Do not let your hands slide as the chest moves, or you will measure your own hand rather than the chest.
  3. A painful chest wall or poor effort looks like reduced expansion, so coach the breath first.
