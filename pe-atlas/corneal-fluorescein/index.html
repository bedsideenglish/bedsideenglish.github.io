Corneal fluorescein · epithelial defects and leak recognition

Show safe dye placement and distinguish focal, linear and punctate epithelial staining from a separate aqueous-leak example.

See the examination

A · Right eye looking up: moistened tip contacts exposed inner lower lid, below and away from the cornea.

B · Separate cobalt-blue view: focal bright-green epithelial defect. The polygon is a schematic example, not an ulcer diagnosis.

C · Independent vertical-track example: consider a subtarsal foreign body; safe lid examination requires a separate technique.

D · Independent punctate-staining example. Distribution is informative but not specific for one disease.

E · Separate leak-recognition example: dark/diluted aqueous stream through bright dye. Do not manipulate a suspected open globe; a negative result cannot exclude it.

How to do it

  1. Record visual acuity and assess trauma, pain, photophobia and contact-lens use first. If open globe is suspected, avoid pressure, lid manipulation and routine staining attempts; arrange urgent specialist assessment. Chemical exposure requires immediate irrigation under the emergency protocol.
  2. With consent and an intact-globe examination appropriate to your training, remove contact lenses if safe. Moisten a sterile single-use fluorescein strip with sterile saline; ask the patient to look up and briefly touch the inside of the lower lid, away from the cornea.
  3. Ask for several blinks to distribute dye. Examine under cobalt-blue illumination and appropriate magnification, preferably a slit lamp. Record the defect’s site, extent and pattern rather than assigning a cause from colour.
  4. Focal bright uptake shows epithelial loss; parallel vertical tracks raise concern for a retained subtarsal foreign body, requiring a separate safe lid examination. Punctate staining has several causes. Pain, reduced vision, an infiltrate or contact-lens-associated disease warrants prompt ophthalmic assessment.
  5. The Seidel panel is leak recognition only: a dark/diluted stream through bright dye suggests aqueous leakage. Do not press or provoke a leak; a sealed, plugged or posterior wound may give a negative result despite open-globe injury.

What to say to the patient

  1. I will touch a moistened dye strip briefly inside your lower lid, then look with blue light. Please look up; tell me if it hurts.

A positive finding

Bright epithelial staining indicates a surface defect, not its cause. A suspected leak or corneal infection needs urgent ophthalmic assessment.

A negative finding

No visible uptake or a negative Seidel appearance does not exclude a foreign body, deeper injury, infection or an open globe.

Evidence & limitations

Original schematic patterns, not slit-lamp photographs. Routine epithelial staining and a clinician’s leak assessment have different indications; the card does not teach provocation of a traumatic wound.

Points to remember

  1. Do not touch or scrape the cornea with the strip. Do not interpret a white corneal opacity as fluorescein uptake; examine it separately.
