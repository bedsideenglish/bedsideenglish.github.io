Blanching response · petechiae and purpura

Describe a rash and compare vascular redness that fades under transparent pressure with extravasated blood that remains visible. This is a physical sign, not a meningitis exclusion test.

How to do it

  1. Assess illness and vital signs first. Rapidly spreading purpura, systemic illness or concerning meningitis features require urgent assessment; do not wait for the rash to become typical.
  2. With consent and appropriate exposure, inspect distribution, lesion size, colour and associated mucosal findings. Look across skin tones and use good lighting; colour alone is unreliable.
  3. On intact skin, gently press a clean transparent examination slide against an area and observe through it. Compare the lesion before, during and after pressure; stop if painful. Avoid injured skin and unprotected mucosal contact.
  4. Record complete, partial or absent blanching. Palpate separately for elevation and tenderness; the pressure illustration does not assess palpable purpura or tissue texture.
  5. Document actual dimensions and evolution rather than assigning cause from colour or size alone. Petechiae and larger purpuric lesions may have several infectious and non-infectious causes.

What to say to the patient

  1. I will press this clear plate gently against the skin to see whether the mark fades. Tell me if it hurts.

A positive finding

A mark persisting under pressure is non-blanching and may represent cutaneous bleeding. Illness, rapid spread or associated red flags determine urgency; this does not identify the organism or cause.

A negative finding

Blanching or no rash does not exclude meningococcal disease, sepsis or another serious disorder. An early rash can change.

Evidence & limitations

The same two response examples are drawn on two schematic skin tones. Rendered colours and spot sizes are not clinical reference standards, calibrated measurements or a complete morphology atlas.

Points to remember

  1. A fading surrounding flare can coexist with a non-blanching centre. Record what remains rather than forcing a binary diagnosis.
