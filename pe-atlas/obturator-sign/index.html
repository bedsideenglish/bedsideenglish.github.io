Obturator sign

Looks for inflammation next to the obturator internus, typically a pelvic appendix or a pelvic abscess. Passive internal rotation of the flexed hip stretches the muscle and reproduces the pain.

How to do it

  1. Patient lies supine and relaxed.
  2. Flex the right hip and the right knee to 90 degrees.
  3. Hold the knee with one hand and the ankle with the other.
  4. Rotate the hip internally by swinging the ankle outward, away from the midline, while the knee stays in place.
  5. Ask about pain in the right lower quadrant or deep in the pelvis. Compare with the left leg.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'm going to bend your right leg and rotate your hip. Is that okay?
  2. Please keep your leg loose and let me move it.
  3. Do you feel any pain deep in your lower belly or pelvis when I turn it?

A positive finding

Pain in the right lower quadrant or pelvis during internal rotation. Suggests a pelvic appendicitis or a pelvic abscess irritating the obturator internus. Hip joint disease can also hurt on rotation.

A negative finding

No pain. Appendicitis is NOT excluded; most patients with appendicitis have a negative obturator sign.

Evidence & limitations

Reported sensitivity is only about 8% with a specificity of roughly 94% in the evidence reviewed by McGee, so the sign is rarely positive and adds little when negative. Decisions rest on the whole picture (migrating pain, anorexia, RLQ tenderness, rebound, WBC) and imaging.

Points to remember

  1. Keep the knee still and move only the ankle; moving the knee sideways is flexion/abduction, not rotation.
  2. Be gentle in a patient with guarding or peritonitis.
  3. A painful hip (arthritis, fracture) gives a false positive, so ask what hurts and where.
