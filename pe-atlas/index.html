  • Homans' signA traditional bedside screen for deep vein thrombosis (DVT) of the calf: calf pain on passive dorsiflexion of the foot. Know it because examiners and patients still mention it, but do not rely on it.Knee, ankle & calf↗
  • Murphy's signLooks for an inflamed gallbladder (acute cholecystitis): on a deep breath the gallbladder descends onto the examiner's fingers, and the pain makes the patient stop breathing in.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Kernig's signA sign of meningeal irritation (meningitis, subarachnoid hemorrhage): with the hip flexed, straightening the knee stretches the inflamed lumbosacral nerve roots, so the patient resists because of pain.Neurological↗
  • Psoas signLooks for an inflamed structure lying on the iliopsoas muscle. A retrocecal appendix touching the right psoas hurts when the muscle is stretched by extending the hip, or when it contracts against resistance.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Obturator signLooks for inflammation next to the obturator internus, typically a pelvic appendix or a pelvic abscess. Passive internal rotation of the flexed hip stretches the muscle and reproduces the pain.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Shifting dullnessDetects free fluid (ascites) in the abdomen. Fluid sinks to the dependent flank when the patient rolls, so a dull note over the flank becomes resonant on the side that is now uppermost, and dull on the side now below.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Jugular venous pressure (JVP) at 45°Estimates central venous pressure (right atrial pressure) from the height of the pulsating column of blood in the internal jugular vein. It is the bedside sign of volume overload and right-sided heart failure.Cardiovascular↗
  • Hepatojugular (abdominojugular) refluxTests whether the right heart can handle a sudden extra load of venous return. Pressure on the right upper abdomen pushes blood toward the heart; a failing or stiff right heart cannot take it, and the JVP rises and stays up.Cardiovascular↗
  • Thyroid palpation from behind, with a swallowFinds goitre and thyroid nodules and describes them: size, consistency, tenderness, whether the gland moves up with swallowing. The thyroid is fixed to the larynx and trachea, so it rises when the patient swallows; a lump that does not rise is probably not thyroid.Head & neck↗
  • Cervical lymph node palpationLooks for enlarged cervical lymph nodes and describes them, which helps separate reactive nodes (infection) from nodes that suggest lymphoma, metastasis or chronic disease. Each chain drains a known area, so the site of a node points to where to look.Head & neck↗
  • Cardiac auscultation areas (A, P, T, M)Listens to the heart sounds and murmurs at four standard sites, each named for the valve whose sound is heard best there. Murmurs often radiate beyond their area, so the area is a guide to where to listen, not proof of which valve is diseased.Cardiovascular↗
  • Left lateral decubitus auscultationRolling the patient onto the left side brings the left ventricular apex against the chest wall, so low-pitched sounds from the left side of the heart become easier to hear: the mitral stenosis rumble, an opening snap, an S3 or S4 gallop.Cardiovascular↗
  • Sitting forward auscultationLeaning forward in held expiration brings the base of the heart and the aortic root closer to the chest wall. It is the position for the soft, high-pitched early diastolic murmur of aortic regurgitation and for a pericardial friction rub.Cardiovascular↗
  • Valsalva maneuver and murmursThe strain phase of the Valsalva maneuver reduces venous return and so lowers left ventricular volume. Most murmurs get softer, but the murmur of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) and the click-murmur of mitral valve prolapse (MVP) get louder or start earlier. It helps separate HCM from aortic stenosis at the bedside.Cardiovascular↗
  • Squat-to-standSquatting increases venous return and afterload, which enlarges the left ventricle; standing quickly does the opposite. Murmurs of HCM and MVP get softer on squatting and louder on standing, the reverse of most other murmurs. Together with Valsalva it is the standard bedside test of dynamic obstruction.Cardiovascular↗
  • Lower-limb pulses (femoral, popliteal, foot)Checks the arterial supply to the legs. Compare both sides at each level: femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis (DP) and posterior tibial (PT). A pulse that is weak or missing from a level down points to arterial narrowing or occlusion at or above that level.Cardiovascular↗
  • Pitting edema gradingTells pitting edema (fluid that can be pressed out of the tissue) from non-pitting swelling, and grades how deep and how slowly the dent recovers. It helps sort out heart, kidney, liver, venous and protein-loss causes of leg swelling.Cardiovascular↗
  • Chest expansion (posterior, thumbs)Compares how much the two sides of the lower chest move during a deep breath. Reduced or delayed movement on one side points to disease of that lung or pleura; reduced movement on both sides points to restrictive or hyperinflated chest disease.Respiratory↗
  • Posterior chest percussion (arms crossed)Uses the note from tapping on the back to tell air-filled lung (resonant) from fluid or solid lung (dull) and from hyperinflation or pneumothorax (hyperresonant). Crossing the arms in front of the chest moves the scapulae out of the way so more lung can be reached.Respiratory↗
  • Lung auscultation sequence (posterior)Listens to breath sounds and added sounds (crackles, wheeze, rhonchi, pleural rub) over all lung zones on the back, in a ladder pattern that compares the left and right side at each level.Respiratory↗
  • Liver edge palpation with inspirationLooks for an enlarged or firm liver. The liver edge moves down on a deep breath and meets the fingertips pressed under the right costal margin, so you can feel its edge, surface and consistency and measure how far it extends below the ribs.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Spleen palpation (right lateral decubitus)Looks for an enlarged spleen. With the patient rolled onto the right side the spleen falls forward and down, so an enlarged tip can meet the examiner's fingertips under the left costal margin during a deep breath in.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Rebound tenderness (Blumberg's sign)Looks for peritoneal irritation. Pressing in slowly and then letting go suddenly makes the inflamed peritoneum snap back, and the patient feels a sharp pain on release that is worse than the pain on pressing.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Rovsing's signAn indirect sign of appendicitis. Pressing in the left lower quadrant pushes gas and bowel contents toward the right, and the stretched inflamed area in the right lower quadrant hurts, even though the pressure was applied on the opposite side.Abdomen & renal↗
  • McBurney's point tendernessTests for localised tenderness over the usual position of the appendix. McBurney's point lies about one third of the way along the line from the right anterior superior iliac spine to the umbilicus.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Light then deep palpation of the abdomenMaps tenderness, guarding and masses across the nine regions of the abdomen. Light palpation finds superficial tenderness and voluntary or involuntary guarding; deep palpation finds organ edges, masses and deeper tenderness.Abdomen & renal↗
  • CVA tenderness (fist percussion)Looks for inflammation or stretch of the kidney capsule. A gentle fist strike over the costovertebral angle (CVA, below the 12th rib beside the spine) hurts when the kidney is inflamed, as in pyelonephritis, or distended by an obstructing stone.Abdomen & renal↗
  • Painful arc (60-120 degrees)Looks for subacromial impingement. As the arm is raised sideways, the rotator cuff tendons and bursa are squeezed under the acromion in the middle of the range, so pain appears between about 60 and 120 degrees and eases above that.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Empty can test (Jobe)Tests the supraspinatus. With the arm raised in the scapular plane and the thumb pointing down, the supraspinatus is the main muscle holding the arm up against a downward push. Weakness suggests a tear or tendon disease; pain without weakness suggests tendinopathy.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Drop arm testLooks for a large or full-thickness rotator cuff tear, mainly the supraspinatus. A shoulder with an intact cuff can hold the arm and lower it smoothly from 90 degrees of abduction; a torn one cannot.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Neer impingement testLooks for subacromial impingement. Raising the arm forward fully, with the arm turned inward and the scapula held still, pushes the greater tuberosity and the supraspinatus tendon against the front of the acromion and reproduces the pain.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Hawkins–Kennedy testScreens for subacromial impingement. With the arm raised to 90 degrees and the elbow bent, passive internal rotation pushes the greater tuberosity and the rotator cuff tendons (mainly supraspinatus) toward the coracoacromial arch and reproduces the pain.Shoulder & arm↗
  • Straight leg raise (with Bragard's sign)Looks for tension on a lumbosacral nerve root, usually an L5 or S1 root irritated by a herniated disc. Raising the straight leg stretches the sciatic nerve; dorsiflexing the ankle adds tension (Bragard's sign) and helps separate nerve pain from tight hamstrings.Spine↗
  • Spurling's testLooks for cervical nerve root compression (cervical radiculopathy). Extending the neck and tilting and turning the head toward the symptomatic side narrows the intervertebral foramen on that side, and a gentle downward press can reproduce arm pain.Spine↗
  • FABER (Patrick's) testProvokes pain from the hip joint and the sacroiliac joint. The hip is flexed, abducted and externally rotated into a figure-4 position (the foot rests on the opposite knee), then the knee is pressed toward the bed while the opposite pelvis is steadied.Hip↗
  • Anterior drawer test (knee)Tests the integrity of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL). With the knee at 90 degrees and the foot fixed, an intact ACL stops the tibia from sliding forward under the femur. An abnormal forward glide with a soft endpoint suggests an ACL tear.Knee, ankle & calf↗
  • Lachman testChecks the integrity of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL). With the knee in slight flexion, the ACL is the main restraint to forward translation of the tibia on the femur, so a torn ACL lets the tibia slide forward with a soft or absent end-point.Knee, ankle & calf↗
  • Thompson (Simmonds) squeeze testTests the continuity of the Achilles tendon. Squeezing a healthy calf pulls on the tendon and plantarflexes the foot; if the tendon is ruptured the pull is not transmitted and the foot does not move.Knee, ankle & calf↗
  • Ottawa ankle and foot rulesA decision rule that tells you whether an acutely injured ankle or midfoot needs an X-ray. You press four bony points and test weight-bearing; if none is positive, a fracture is very unlikely and imaging can be skipped.Knee, ankle & calf↗
  • Brudzinski's neck signA sign of meningeal irritation. Bending the neck forward stretches the inflamed meninges and nerve roots, so the patient flexes the hips and knees reflexly to relieve the traction.Neurological↗
  • Lower-limb power by nerve root (L2-S1)Tests muscle power joint by joint, each linked to a main nerve root, so a pattern of weakness points to a level: hip flexion L2, knee extension L3-L4, ankle dorsiflexion L4-L5, ankle plantarflexion S1. Power is graded 0 to 5 on the MRC scale.Neurological↗
  • Romberg testTests whether balance depends on proprioception (dorsal columns, peripheral nerves) rather than on vision. Standing steadily needs two of three inputs: vision, proprioception and the vestibular-cerebellar system. Closing the eyes removes vision, so a patient with a position-sense deficit starts to fall.Neurological↗
  • Finger-to-nose testTests upper-limb coordination. The patient alternately touches the examiner's finger and their own nose. A cerebellar lesion makes the movement overshoot (dysmetria) and the hand shake as it nears the target (intention tremor).Neurological↗
  • Heel-to-shin testTests lower-limb coordination. The patient puts one heel on the opposite knee and slides it smoothly down the shin to the ankle. A cerebellar lesion makes the heel wander off the shin (ataxia).Neurological↗
  • Dix-Hallpike maneuverDiagnoses posterior-canal benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV). Turning the head and lying the patient back with the head hanging moves loose otoconia in the posterior canal and provokes vertigo with a characteristic burst of nystagmus.Hearing & balance↗
  • Head impulse test (HINTS)Checks the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) in a patient with acute continuous vertigo. A quick small head turn should keep the eyes locked on the target. If a catch-up saccade is needed, the VOR on that side is impaired, which points to a peripheral vestibular lesion; a normal impulse in a patient with acute vestibular syndrome is worrying for stroke.Hearing & balance↗
