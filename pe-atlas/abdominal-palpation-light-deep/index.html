Light then deep palpation of the abdomen

Maps tenderness, guarding and masses across the nine regions of the abdomen. Light palpation finds superficial tenderness and voluntary or involuntary guarding; deep palpation finds organ edges, masses and deeper tenderness.

How to do it

  1. Warm your hands, tell the patient what you will do, and position the patient flat with the knees bent and arms at the sides. Ask where the pain is and begin as far from it as possible.
  2. Light palpation: with the flat of the hand and fingers together, press about 1 cm with a gentle dipping motion into each of the nine regions in turn. Watch the face, not the hand.
  3. Note tenderness, guarding (voluntary, then involuntary rigidity), superficial masses and a pulsatile or hernial swelling.
  4. Deep palpation: repeat in each region, pressing about 4 to 5 cm with the pads of the fingers, after the patient breathes out. Feel for masses, the liver and spleen edges, the kidneys and the aorta.
  5. Examine the painful region last, then add rebound, Murphy's sign and other special tests if indicated. Compare the two sides.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'm going to feel your belly. Please let me know if anything is tender, and I'll start away from the sore area.
  2. Please try to relax and breathe normally with your knees bent.
  3. Now I'll press a bit deeper. Tell me if it hurts or if it feels different from the other side.

A positive finding

Localised tenderness points to the underlying organ (RUQ gallbladder, epigastric stomach/pancreas, RLQ appendix, LLQ sigmoid, suprapubic bladder or uterus). Involuntary guarding or rigidity suggests peritonitis. A mass, an organ edge or a pulsatile mass needs further assessment and imaging.

A negative finding

A soft, non-tender abdomen with no masses. This is reassuring, but early appendicitis, retroperitoneal disease and small bowel obstruction can still be present; correlate with the history and consider imaging.

Evidence & limitations

Palpation findings are interpreted as part of the whole examination: localised tenderness over the site of an organ and abdominal rigidity (LR about 3.8 for appendicitis in the Wagner review) are the most useful; there are few trials of the light-versus-deep sequence itself. Inter-examiner agreement for guarding and tenderness is fair, and for masses and organomegaly lower.

Points to remember

  1. Cold hands, a rushed approach or a full bladder cause guarding; relax the patient first, and distract with conversation.
  2. Voluntary guarding relaxes on breathing out. Rigidity does not relax.
  3. Do not press first on the painful area; look at the face, not at your hands.
