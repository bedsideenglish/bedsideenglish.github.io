Brudzinski's neck sign

A sign of meningeal irritation. Bending the neck forward stretches the inflamed meninges and nerve roots, so the patient flexes the hips and knees reflexly to relieve the traction.

How to do it

  1. Patient lies supine with the legs relaxed and straight, without a pillow if possible.
  2. Place one hand behind the patient's head (occiput) and the other flat on the chest to keep the trunk down.
  3. Slowly flex the neck so the chin moves toward the chest.
  4. Watch the hips and knees (and the face). Positive: both hips and knees flex on their own as the neck bends.
  5. Also check for neck stiffness (resistance to flexion) and pair it with Kernig's sign. Do not test if cervical spine injury is suspected.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'd like to check your neck. Please lie flat and relax.
  2. I'm going to gently bend your head forward so your chin comes toward your chest.
  3. Tell me if you feel pain in your neck, head or back.

A positive finding

Hips and knees flex involuntarily when the neck is flexed. This supports meningeal irritation (meningitis, subarachnoid hemorrhage) and calls for urgent action: blood cultures, empirical antibiotics and imaging or lumbar puncture as indicated.

A negative finding

The legs stay still. This does NOT rule out meningitis; most patients with meningitis have a negative Brudzinski's sign.

Evidence & limitations

In a prospective study of adults with suspected meningitis, Brudzinski's sign had a sensitivity of about 5 percent and a specificity of about 95 percent, the same as Kernig's sign. A later study also found poor sensitivity. A negative result must never delay antibiotics or lumbar puncture.

Points to remember

  1. Never flex the neck when cervical spine injury or severe cervical disease is possible.
  2. Neck pain from arthritis or a muscle strain can make the patient resist; look for hip and knee flexion, not only neck pain.
  3. Move slowly and warn the patient first; patients with meningitis often have photophobia and severe headache.
  4. The sign is also called the neck sign; Brudzinski described other signs (such as the contralateral leg sign) that are rarely tested.
