Plantar response: flexor versus extensor (Babinski)

Elicit the plantar response with a controlled lateral-sole stroke and identify the first sustained movement of the great toe.

How to do it

  1. Explain, obtain consent and lie the patient supine with the leg relaxed. Support the ankle and inspect the sole for wounds or reasons to avoid the stimulus.
  2. Use a blunt, non-injuring probe. Start gently near the lateral heel, stroke forward along the lateral sole toward the base of the fifth toe, then curve medially across the forefoot, stopping short of the toe pads.
  3. Watch the great toe rather than just the whole foot. Distinguish plantar flexion, slow extension, no clear movement and rapid withdrawal; repeat on the other side.
  4. Describe the actual response and symmetry. Interpret an extensor response with other upper-motor-neuron findings and the patient’s age and state.

What to say to the patient

  1. I will stroke the outside of the sole of your foot with a blunt tool. Please let your leg relax.
  2. Tell me if this hurts; I will avoid scratching the skin.

A positive finding

A reproducible slow extensor great-toe response in an adult supports corticospinal dysfunction; other toes may fan, but fanning alone does not define Babinski. Interpret in context, including altered consciousness or a recent seizure.

A negative finding

A flexor response does not exclude a corticospinal lesion. An absent, equivocal or withdrawal response should be documented as such rather than called a definite normal reflex.

Evidence & limitations

This qualitative sign is technique- and observer-dependent. The side inset contrasts toe flexion and extension; it is not a severity scale or diagnostic-accuracy claim.

Points to remember

  1. Do not use a sharp pin, repeatedly scrape, or provoke an overwhelming withdrawal. Withdrawal can make the toe response uninterpretable.
  2. The right sole is viewed from below: medial is screen-left. The stroke stops before the toes. Response panels are separate hypothetical examples, not successive responses in one patient.
