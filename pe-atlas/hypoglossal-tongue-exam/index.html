CN XII · tongue at rest, protrusion and lateral movement

Separate observation of tongue bulk and involuntary activity from movement tasks, and describe deviation using the patient’s side.

See the examination

A · Inspect the relaxed tongue inside the mouth before asking for movement. No simulated twitch frequency is shown.

B · Normal task: protrude toward the facial midline. Patient R is screen-left and L is screen-right.

C · Independent example: protrusion toward patient-right may fit right lower motor weakness; it does not establish a lesion by itself.

D · Separate movement task toward patient-left; the shape is not a different disease stage.

E · Separate movement task toward patient-right. Assess control and range in the actual examination.

How to do it

  1. Explain, obtain permission and use adequate light. Inspect the tongue relaxed inside the open mouth for asymmetry, wasting and fasciculations; distinguish brief whole-tongue movement or tremor from fasciculation.
  2. Ask for straight protrusion without exaggerated effort. Compare the tongue tip with the facial midline and document direction as patient-right or patient-left.
  3. Ask the patient to move the tongue toward each side. Compare range and control, and assess speech and the rest of the cranial nerve examination as indicated.
  4. A lower motor hypoglossal deficit can cause protrusion toward the weaker side, often with other lower motor findings. Do not apply this simplification to every central, bilateral or mechanical cause of deviation.
  5. New acute tongue weakness with speech, swallowing or other neurological change requires prompt assessment. Do not delay to reproduce every panel.

What to say to the patient

  1. Open your mouth and let your tongue rest. Now stick it straight out, then move it to your left and right.

A positive finding

Wasting, fasciculation, weakness or persistent deviation can support a neurological abnormality; localization requires the accompanying findings.

A negative finding

Symmetric protrusion alone does not exclude bulbar, central or neuromuscular disease.

Evidence & limitations

The right-weakness panel is an independent lower motor example, not an animation of normal into disease. Normal movement to each side is a separate task.

Points to remember

  1. Always state patient side; a deviating tip is not a complete stroke-localization rule.
