McMurray test — supported tibial rotation

Assess meniscal symptoms with controlled knee flexion/extension and tibial rotation, using the external-rotation/valgus and internal-rotation/varus variant.

How to do it

  1. Explain, examine joint-line tenderness and check the injured knee. Do not force a locked, very painful or acutely unstable knee.
  2. With the patient supine, support the heel with one hand and cup the knee with the other, palpating the joint line. Flex the knee as tolerated.
  3. For the medial side, externally rotate the tibia relative to the femur and apply gentle valgus while slowly extending the knee through the tolerated range.
  4. Return to supported flexion. For the lateral side, use internal tibial rotation with gentle varus while extending.
  5. Record familiar joint-line pain and a palpable/audible click or clunk with its location; stop for pain or catching rather than repeating forcefully.
  6. Interpret with the history and other examination findings; consider imaging/referral according to the clinical presentation.

What to say to the patient

  1. Let me support your knee and heel; keep the leg relaxed.
  2. Tell me if this brings back your usual joint pain or catching.

A positive finding

Familiar joint-line pain with a palpable/audible click or clunk increases suspicion of a meniscal lesion. Rotation direction guides the intended compartment but does not establish an exact tear site.

A negative finding

A negative McMurray test cannot exclude a tear; pain, guarding and incomplete range reduce interpretability.

Evidence & limitations

AAOS 2024 supports combined physical examination in acute isolated meniscal injury. NIHR primary-care research found limited stand-alone accuracy. The populations and positive-test definitions differ; no single universal likelihood ratio is assigned.

Points to remember

  1. Turn the tibia relative to the femur; rotating the entire hip is not a faithful substitute.
  2. A painless incidental click alone is not equivalent to symptomatic meniscal disease.
  3. Side-view posture and distal-axis inset are separate projections. Arrows indicate applied direction, not simulated tissue gapping or a tear.
