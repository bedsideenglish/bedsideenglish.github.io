Spleen palpation (right lateral decubitus)

Looks for an enlarged spleen. With the patient rolled onto the right side the spleen falls forward and down, so an enlarged tip can meet the examiner's fingertips under the left costal margin during a deep breath in.

How to do it

  1. Start with the patient supine and palpate first, beginning low in the right lower quadrant and moving toward the left costal margin so that a very large spleen is not missed.
  2. Roll the patient onto the right side, with the hips and knees slightly flexed and the left arm resting forward.
  3. Place your left hand behind the left lower ribs and pull them gently forward. Place the fingertips of your right hand below the left costal margin, pointing toward the left armpit.
  4. Ask for a slow deep breath in. Keep the fingers still and let the spleen tip descend onto them; press in a little more after each breath out.
  5. If you feel the edge, note how far it extends below the costal margin, its firmness, and any notch. Repeat with the patient supine.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'd like to feel your belly on the left side to check your spleen. Please roll onto your right side.
  2. Take a slow, deep breath in through your mouth, and then let it out.
  3. Does it hurt anywhere when I press here?

A positive finding

A firm edge that moves down on inspiration, often with a notch on its medial border, and that you cannot get your fingers above (unlike a kidney). Splenomegaly is likely: think of portal hypertension, haematological disease (lymphoma, leukaemia, myeloproliferative disease), infection (mononucleosis, endocarditis, malaria) and infiltration. Confirm with ultrasound.

A negative finding

No edge felt. A normal spleen is not palpable, but a mildly enlarged spleen is often missed, so a negative palpation does not exclude splenomegaly. Percuss (Castell or Traube) or use ultrasound if the suspicion is high.

Evidence & limitations

In the systematic review by Grover and colleagues, a palpable spleen made splenomegaly much more likely (positive LR roughly 8), while the sensitivity of palpation varied widely between studies (roughly 30 to 80%) with high specificity (roughly 90 to 99%). The extra gain from the right lateral position over supine palpation is small. Ultrasound or CT is far more accurate.

Points to remember

  1. Start low. A massively enlarged spleen reaches the right iliac fossa, and starting under the ribs would skip it.
  2. Do not poke at the fingertips; hold still and let the spleen come to you on inspiration.
  3. A normal spleen tip may be palpable in a thin young person; also separate the spleen from a left kidney mass or stool in the colon.
