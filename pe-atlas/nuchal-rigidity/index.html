Nuchal rigidity — gentle passive neck flexion

Assess abnormal resistance to gentle passive neck flexion as one possible sign of meningeal irritation. This is an adjunct to the overall assessment of an adult with a concerning illness, not a test that can clear meningitis.

How to do it

  1. First assess the patient's overall condition, vital signs and level of consciousness. Ask about trauma, known cervical instability, recent neck surgery or severe focal neck pain; if injury or instability is possible, protect the neck and assess it rather than attempting passive flexion.
  2. Explain the gentle movement and obtain consent. Position the patient supine with the shoulders relaxed. Ask them to let the examiner support and move the head.
  3. Cradle the occiput with one hand. Rest the other hand lightly on the upper chest to observe trunk movement; do not pin the chest or pull on the jaw.
  4. Slowly guide the head into forward flexion towards the chest, feeling for involuntary resistance. Stop at pain or resistance; do not force the chin to reach the chest or use the drawing's angle as a target.
  5. Support the head back to rest. Record resistance, pain, range limitation and whether the examination was reliable. Involuntary hip/knee flexion is a separate Brudzinski response, not required to find nuchal rigidity.
  6. Interpret with the history and neurological/systemic findings. Suspected meningitis requires prompt clinical assessment and management even if the neck is supple; do not delay care by repeatedly eliciting signs.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'll support your head and gently bring it forwards. Is that all right?
  2. Please relax your neck and let me do the moving.
  3. Tell me straight away if it hurts. I will stop if there is pain or resistance.

A positive finding

Abnormal involuntary resistance to gentle neck flexion supports a meningeal-irritation finding in context. It does not identify infection or its cause. Cervical disease, muscle spasm and guarding can also limit movement; pain alone should not be relabelled as proven meningeal rigidity.

A negative finding

A supple neck does NOT rule out meningitis, including early illness. A limited or unreliable examination is not a negative result. Absence of stiffness may be less reassuring in older or immunocompromised adults; this adult technique should not be extrapolated to infants.

Evidence & limitations

In a prospective study of 297 adults evaluated for suspected meningitis, nuchal rigidity had only 30% sensitivity for CSF pleocytosis. This is not a universal estimate for bacterial meningitis, but it illustrates why absence cannot be used alone to exclude disease. Current guidance considers the overall symptom/sign pattern.

Points to remember

  1. Never overcome resistance or repeatedly provoke pain. A possible cervical injury requires a protected assessment, not a meningism maneuver.
  2. Do not interpret all restricted neck motion as meningitis, or a negative Kernig/Brudzinski/nuchal-rigidity cluster as safe exclusion in a clinically concerning illness.
  3. The figure demonstrates supported flexion and release, not the force or tactile resistance a clinician feels. The neck angle is illustrative, not a required endpoint or severity scale. The legs remain resting to distinguish this teaching point from Brudzinski's sign.
