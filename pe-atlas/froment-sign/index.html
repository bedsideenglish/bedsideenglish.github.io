Froment's sign — lateral paper pinch

Look for thumb IP flexion used to compensate for weak ulnar-innervated adductor pollicis during a lateral pinch.

How to do it

  1. Explain the comparison and check that thumb pain or joint restriction will not prevent a useful test.
  2. Place a strip of paper between the thumb and the radial side of the index finger, using a key pinch rather than a fingertip-to-fingertip pinch.
  3. Ask the patient to keep the thumb IP joint straight and retain the paper while you gently pull it distally along the thumb.
  4. Watch the IP joint and compare both hands. Record paper loss, IP flexion, pain and other compensations separately.
  5. Add finger abduction/adduction, ulnar-distribution sensation and a broader neurologic examination to localise the problem.

What to say to the patient

  1. Hold this paper against the side of your index finger.
  2. Try to keep the end joint of your thumb straight while I gently pull.

A positive finding

Thumb IP flexion during the task is a positive Froment sign: FPL (median/anterior interosseous nerve) substitutes for weak thumb adduction. It does not by itself localise an ulnar lesion to the elbow or wrist.

A negative finding

A straight IP joint does not exclude early ulnar neuropathy; pain, technique and baseline thumb mobility affect performance.

Evidence & limitations

This is a motor substitution sign, not a grip-strength grade. No universal sensitivity or force threshold is assigned.

Points to remember

  1. IP flexion is Froment; MCP hyperextension is the distinct Jeanne sign.
  2. Do not start with the thumb tip already hooked onto the paper.
  3. Normal and IP-flexion panels are separate teaching examples, not a simulated patient changing from healthy to diseased.
