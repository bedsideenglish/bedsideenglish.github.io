Femoral nerve stretch — prone knee bend

Look for reproduction of familiar anterior-thigh radicular pain by gently bending the knee in prone. This mainly assesses the upper lumbar/femoral nerve pathway, commonly L2–L4; it does not by itself locate a lesion.

How to do it

  1. Ask about the usual pain distribution and obtain consent. Check for an acute injury, painful knee/hip disease or a recent operation that makes passive movement unsuitable. Explain that the patient can stop the examination at any time.
  2. Position the patient prone and comfortable, with the pelvis and thigh supported. Compare the less symptomatic side first when possible. This card uses the knee-bend variant with the hip kept neutral.
  3. Support the distal leg near the ankle with one hand and steady the pelvis gently with the other. Ask the patient to relax; the examiner performs the movement.
  4. Slowly flex the knee within a comfortable range while keeping the thigh on the couch and avoiding pelvic lift or lumbar extension. Ask where the sensation is felt and whether it matches the presenting pain.
  5. Stop at familiar radiating pain, marked discomfort or resistance. Do not force the heel to the buttock or add a thigh lift to this version. Lower the leg with support.
  6. Repeat on the other side as appropriate. Record the side, pain location and response, then correlate with hip flexion/knee-extension strength, patellar reflex and sensation.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'll support your leg and gently bend your knee while you lie on your front.
  2. Please let me do the moving. Tell me where you feel anything, and whether it is your usual pain.
  3. Tell me straight away if it hurts or you would like me to stop.

A positive finding

Reproduction of the familiar radiating pain in the anterior thigh supports irritation of an upper lumbar/femoral nerve pathway in the clinical context. An isolated stretch in the quadriceps, local knee pain or nonspecific back discomfort is not equivalent to this radicular response.

A negative finding

No familiar anterior-thigh pain does not exclude L2–L4 root involvement or femoral neuropathy. Pain-limited positioning or an incomplete examination should be recorded as limited, not as a confidently negative test.

Evidence & limitations

Studies of midlumbar root impingement support using femoral stretch alongside neurological findings. Accuracy depends on the population, technique and reference diagnosis; a negative provocation test is insufficient to exclude radiculopathy. No single knee angle is a diagnostic cutoff here.

Points to remember

  1. Do not confuse this with an Ely test: pelvic lift or hip flexion during knee bending may reflect rectus-femoris tightness rather than a radicular pain response.
  2. Variants add hip extension; this illustration deliberately keeps the hip neutral. Do not blend variants when reporting the result.
  3. The picture demonstrates right-sided handling and a possible symptom location, not a nerve lesion or measured tension. The angles are illustrative; stop earlier if symptoms occur. Compare both sides in practice.
