Buerger postural colour test

Observe elevation pallor and dependent rubor as adjuncts to lower-limb arterial assessment, not a test for Buerger disease specifically.

See the examination

A · Supine: support the straight leg at approximately 45 degrees.

B · Separate example: pallor during elevation; compare actual feet.

C · Assisted sitting: observe dependent colour; rubor is an abnormal example.

How to do it

  1. Explain; examine both feet at baseline in good light, including temperature, wounds, pulses and symptoms.
  2. With the patient supine, support both legs with knees straight and raise them to about 45 degrees. Observe colour and superficial venous emptying; a commonly described method observes for about one minute if tolerated.
  3. Lower safely and help the patient sit with feet dependent over the bed edge. Observe colour return and any dusky red colour; use a real timer if recording time.
  4. Return to a comfortable position; document side, symptoms and actual colour response. New severe pain, coldness, weakness or an acutely threatened limb requires urgent vascular assessment.
  5. Use objective testing such as Doppler pressures/ABI or toe pressures as indicated; do not grade arterial stenosis from the illustration.

What to say to the patient

  1. I will support your legs, then help you sit up. Tell me immediately if this hurts.

A positive finding

Elevation pallor followed by dependent dusky rubor supports arterial insufficiency in context; it does not quantify obstruction or establish its cause.

A negative finding

An unremarkable colour response does not exclude PAD; skin pigmentation, lighting, venous disease and ambient temperature complicate interpretation.

Evidence & limitations

Traditional bedside adjunct with limited diagnostic accuracy. The drawn angle, colour and playback duration are not validated perfusion thresholds.

Points to remember

  1. Do not persist with painful elevation or require an unsafe sit-up.
  2. Only one near leg is drawn for clarity; inspect and compare both. Pallor and rubor are separate abnormal examples, not an inevitable sequence.
