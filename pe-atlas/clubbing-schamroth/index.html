Clubbing and Schamroth's window

Inspect nail-fold profile and distal finger shape, then look for the window between opposed corresponding fingernails.

How to do it

  1. Explain the examination and inspect both hands in good light. Look at the nails from the side for loss of the normal nail-fold angle and increased distal tissue bulk.
  2. Ask the patient to oppose the dorsal surfaces of the corresponding index-finger distal phalanges, with nails facing each other. Bring them together gently without force.
  3. View the contact from the side. A small diamond-shaped gap normally appears near the proximal nail folds; check whether it remains or is obliterated.
  4. Confirm a suspected finding with the overall nail profile, distal phalangeal depth and clinical history. Record whether it is symmetrical or localised.

What to say to the patient

  1. Could you place the backs of your index fingertips together, with your nails facing each other?
  2. Touch them lightly; you do not need to squeeze.

A positive finding

Loss of the window together with an altered nail-fold profile and distal bulbous enlargement supports clubbing. Acquired clubbing can accompany thoracic, cardiac, gastrointestinal and other disorders; the sign does not identify one cause.

A negative finding

A visible window makes established clubbing less apparent but does not exclude early change or the underlying diseases being considered.

Evidence & limitations

Schamroth is a bedside inspection sign, not a disease-specific test. The paired profiles are conceptual examples, without measured nail angles or an invented diagnostic-accuracy estimate.

Points to remember

  1. Poor alignment, thick or deformed nails and forced pressure can make the gap misleading. Do not diagnose clubbing from the window alone.
  2. The two panels compare ordinary and clubbed profiles; they do not depict a finger becoming clubbed during the examination. Nail-bed sponginess requires palpation and is not simulated.
