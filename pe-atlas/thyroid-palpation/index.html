Thyroid palpation from behind, with a swallow

Finds goitre and thyroid nodules and describes them: size, consistency, tenderness, whether the gland moves up with swallowing. The thyroid is fixed to the larynx and trachea, so it rises when the patient swallows; a lump that does not rise is probably not thyroid.

How to do it

  1. Seat the patient on a stool or the bed edge; inspect the front of the neck, then give the patient a glass of water.
  2. Stand behind the patient. Ask for a slight neck flexion to relax the strap muscles.
  3. Place the fingers of both hands just below the cricoid cartilage, with the thumbs at the back of the neck. Find the isthmus first, then each lobe lateral to the trachea.
  4. Ask the patient to take a sip of water and swallow. Feel the gland rise under the fingers and note any nodule, its size and consistency.
  5. Palpate each lobe separately by pushing the trachea slightly to that side. Then check for a bruit with the stethoscope and look for cervical nodes.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'd like to feel your thyroid gland in your neck. Could you sit up and tip your chin down a little?
  2. I'm going to put my hands around your neck from behind. Take a sip of water and hold it in your mouth.
  3. Now swallow for me, please.

A positive finding

A diffusely enlarged smooth gland (goitre), a discrete nodule, a firm or hard irregular lump, or a gland that is tender. Diffuse goitre with a bruit suggests Graves disease; a tender gland suggests subacute thyroiditis; a single hard nodule, fixation to the trachea, a hoarse voice or cervical nodes raise concern for cancer and need ultrasound and FNA.

A negative finding

A gland that is not felt or is only just palpable and rises smoothly on swallowing is normal on examination. A normal examination does not exclude small nodules, which are very common and mostly benign.

Evidence & limitations

Palpation is not very sensitive: a clinically normal gland can hide nodules that ultrasound finds in a large share of adults, and examiners often disagree about size and nodularity. Only nodules larger than about 1 cm are reliably felt. Guidelines therefore base the work-up (ultrasound, TSH, FNA) on clinical suspicion together with imaging rather than on palpation alone.

Points to remember

  1. Palpating from the front for the first look only; the standard method uses both hands from behind (or one thumb and finger from the front).
  2. Pressing hard on the carotids, which can cause a vagal reflex. Keep the pressure gentle.
  3. Calling a midline lump thyroid without a swallow: a thyroglossal cyst rises with tongue protrusion, the thyroid with swallowing.
