Romberg test

Tests whether balance depends on proprioception (dorsal columns, peripheral nerves) rather than on vision. Standing steadily needs two of three inputs: vision, proprioception and the vestibular-cerebellar system. Closing the eyes removes vision, so a patient with a position-sense deficit starts to fall.

How to do it

  1. Stand close to the patient so you can catch a fall. Ask the patient to stand with feet together, arms by the sides or held forward, eyes open.
  2. Watch for 20 to 30 seconds. If the patient already sways or falls with eyes open, the problem is cerebellar or vestibular, and this is not a Romberg test.
  3. Ask the patient to close the eyes, and keep watching for another 20 to 30 seconds. Keep your hands near the shoulders without touching.
  4. Positive: the patient is steady with eyes open but loses balance, or must step, once the eyes are closed.
  5. Optionally give a light nudge on the sternum or shoulders to see how well balance recovers.

What to say to the patient

  1. Please stand with your feet together and your arms by your sides. I'll stand right next to you.
  2. Now close your eyes and try to stand still. I won't let you fall.
  3. You can open your eyes now. Thank you.

A positive finding

Stable with eyes open, unstable with eyes closed. Loss of proprioceptive input is likely: dorsal column disease (tabes dorsalis, subacute combined degeneration from B12 deficiency) or a sensory neuropathy. Check vibration and joint position sense to confirm.

A negative finding

Steady with eyes closed. Position sense is probably adequate for standing, but a mild deficit or a cerebellar or vestibular disorder is not excluded. If the patient is unsteady with eyes open, this points to a cerebellar or vestibular cause, not a positive Romberg.

Evidence & limitations

Romberg is a bedside screen of limited accuracy, and good sensitivity and specificity data against a strict reference standard are lacking, so treat it as supportive. It is most useful when the history already suggests a sensory ataxia (numb feet, worse in the dark).

Points to remember

  1. Stay close and guard the patient; falls are the main risk, especially in older adults.
  2. Do not call it positive if the patient already sways with eyes open: that is cerebellar or vestibular ataxia.
  3. Pain, weakness, or poor vision can also upset standing balance; interpret with the rest of the neurological exam.
