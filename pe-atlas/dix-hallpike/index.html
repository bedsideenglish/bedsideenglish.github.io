Dix-Hallpike maneuver

Diagnoses posterior-canal benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV). Turning the head and lying the patient back with the head hanging moves loose otoconia in the posterior canal and provokes vertigo with a characteristic burst of nystagmus.

How to do it

  1. Explain the test, check the neck and back are safe, and sit the patient on the bed with enough room to lie back with the head hanging.
  2. Turn the head 45 degrees toward the ear being tested and hold it with both hands. Ask the patient to keep the eyes open and fixed on your forehead.
  3. Keeping the head turned, bring the patient quickly from sitting to lying with the head hanging about 20 degrees below horizontal.
  4. Watch the eyes for up to 30 to 60 seconds. Ask whether the vertigo is reproduced.
  5. Sit the patient up and watch again. Repeat on the other side.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'm going to lay you back quickly. It may make you dizzy, but it passes. Please keep your eyes open and look at me.
  2. Tell me if the room spins, and keep looking at my forehead.
  3. Now I'll sit you up slowly. Take your time.

A positive finding

Vertigo with torsional, upbeat nystagmus (the top of the eye beats toward the lower ear) after a delay of a few seconds, building up and fading within about a minute, and weaker on repeat. This is typical of posterior-canal BPPV; treat with the Epley maneuver.

A negative finding

No vertigo or nystagmus. Posterior-canal BPPV is less likely on that side, but it does not exclude BPPV of another canal (use the supine roll test for the horizontal canal), and a single negative test can be repeated. Persistent nystagmus or a pattern that does not fatigue points to a central cause.

Evidence & limitations

In a critically appraised review (Halker 2008) the Dix-Hallpike test had a pooled sensitivity of about 79% and specificity of about 75% for BPPV, but the studies were few and varied. A positive test with typical nystagmus is strong enough to start treatment.

Points to remember

  1. Take care, or avoid it, in a patient with a recent neck injury, severe cervical spine disease, or a vascular problem of the neck.
  2. Atypical findings (no latency, direction-changing or vertical downbeat nystagmus, nystagmus that does not fatigue, or neurological signs) suggest a central cause: image.
  3. Frenzel goggles make nystagmus easier to see, but without them have the patient fix on your forehead and watch for the torsional beat.
