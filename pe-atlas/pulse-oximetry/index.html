Pulse oximetry · placement, signal and limitations

Estimate peripheral oxygen saturation while checking whether the optical pulse signal is trustworthy. Oxygenation and ventilation require separate assessment.

See the examination

B · Optical and palpated pulse marks align on a shared conceptual timeline. Check actual signal quality and concordance; these are not measured pulses or a rate scale.

C · Movement, cold/low flow, coatings and poor fit can corrupt the signal; address the cause and reassess.

D · Pigmentation and abnormal hemoglobins can mislead. Saturation does not measure CO2; select confirmatory testing by context.

How to do it

  1. Explain the measurement; assess breathing, circulation and awareness immediately if unwell. Use a medical-purpose device and an appropriate sensor/site according to its instructions.
  2. Support a warm, still hand. Check nail coatings and sensor fit; in a transmission finger clip the emitter and detector face each other across the tissue. Avoid a constricted, injured or poorly perfused site.
  3. Wait for a stable reading and adequate signal-quality indication. Compare displayed pulse rate with the palpated pulse or appropriate monitored heart rate. A plausible number alone is insufficient.
  4. Record measured SpO2, pulse, site, signal quality, time and room air or oxygen device/flow. With discordant symptoms, correct movement, cold, fit or interference, reassess and confirm by an appropriate method.
  5. Darker pigmentation can contribute to overestimation; there is no bedside skin-colour correction formula. Suspected CO exposure or methemoglobinemia needs appropriate co-oximetry; blood-gas assessment of ventilation is separate. Do not delay urgent care to obtain a reassuring number.

What to say to the patient

  1. Please keep your hand relaxed and still while I check the signal and compare your pulse.

A positive finding

A low or falling estimate, unreliable signal or mismatch with the clinical condition requires prompt reassessment and context-appropriate confirmation.

A negative finding

A normal displayed SpO2 does not exclude hypoventilation, hypercapnia, CO poisoning or clinically important hypoxemia masked by measurement bias.

Evidence & limitations

Two-wavelength pulse oximetry estimates saturation; it neither measures PaCO2 nor identifies all abnormal hemoglobins. Optical placement and pulse-concordance marks are conceptual, not a calibration or recorded patient waveform.

Points to remember

  1. Do not apply a numerical correction for race or colour, or dismiss symptoms because the displayed saturation is normal.
