Jugular venous pressure (JVP) at 45°

Estimates central venous pressure (right atrial pressure) from the height of the pulsating column of blood in the internal jugular vein. It is the bedside sign of volume overload and right-sided heart failure.

How to do it

  1. Raise the head of the bed to about 45° and have the patient relax the neck muscles with the head resting on a pillow.
  2. Turn the head slightly (about 30–45°) away from the side you examine, and use tangential light across the right side of the neck.
  3. Find the internal jugular pulsation behind the sternocleidomastoid; it is double-waved, not palpable, and falls with inspiration. Confirm with gentle pressure above the clavicle (it fills from above) or by sitting the patient up.
  4. Find the sternal angle (angle of Louis) and the top of the pulsating column. Lay a ruler horizontally from the top of the column and measure the vertical height above the sternal angle.
  5. Add about 5 cm (the right atrium lies about 5 cm below the sternal angle). A height of more than about 3–4 cm above the angle, so a CVP above about 8–9 cm H2O, is raised.

What to say to the patient

  1. I'd like to look at the veins in your neck. Could you lean back on the pillow?
  2. Please turn your head a little to the left and relax your neck.
  3. Breathe normally. I'm going to shine a light across your neck.

A positive finding

Elevated JVP (column more than about 3–4 cm above the sternal angle at 45°): raised right atrial pressure. Think right or left heart failure, volume overload, pericardial tamponade or constrictive pericarditis, tricuspid disease, pulmonary hypertension or massive PE. Look for Kussmaul sign (a rise with inspiration) and prominent a or v waves.

A negative finding

A flat or low JVP (pulsation only just above the clavicle, or not seen when lying flat) suggests normal or low volume. It does not exclude left-sided heart failure. A column that cannot be seen at any angle can also be due to a short thick neck, so say that you could not assess it rather than calling it normal.

Evidence & limitations

In published series an elevated JVP found by experienced examiners favours a raised central venous pressure with a large positive likelihood ratio (roughly 4 to 10), and an apparently normal JVP lowers the probability. Accuracy depends strongly on the examiner and on body habitus, and trainees often misjudge the height, so confirm doubtful findings with ultrasound of the IVC or jugular veins.

Points to remember

  1. Mistaking the carotid pulse for the JVP: the carotid is palpable, has a single sharp beat and does not change with position or breathing.
  2. Measuring at the wrong angle: the zero point is the sternal angle, and the vertical (not the along-the-skin) distance counts. If the column is higher than the jaw at 45°, sit the patient up; if you cannot see it, lower the bed.
  3. Turning the head too far or pressing on the neck, which collapses the vein or distorts the column.
