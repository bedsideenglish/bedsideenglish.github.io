Posterior chest percussion (arms crossed)

Uses the note from tapping on the back to tell air-filled lung (resonant) from fluid or solid lung (dull) and from hyperinflation or pneumothorax (hyperresonant). Crossing the arms in front of the chest moves the scapulae out of the way so more lung can be reached.

How to do it

  1. Sit the patient upright and ask them to fold their arms across the chest and lean slightly forward, so the shoulder blades move apart.
  2. Stand behind the patient. Lay the middle finger of your non-dominant hand flat on an intercostal space, with only the finger pad touching the skin.
  3. Strike the end joint of that finger with the tip of the middle finger of your other hand, using a quick wrist movement that bounces off immediately.
  4. Percuss from the apex down, one space at a time, always comparing the right and the left at the same level, and stay medial to the scapulae and spine.
  5. Find the lower border (the diaphragm) at the end of a normal breath, then ask for a deep breath in and out and mark how far it moves (normally about 4 to 6 cm).

What to say to the patient

  1. I'm going to tap on your back to listen to the lungs. Please sit up and cross your arms in front of you.
  2. Lean forward a little. Nothing will hurt.
  3. Take a deep breath in and hold it... Now breathe out.

A positive finding

A dull (stony dull) note suggests pleural effusion or consolidation or a collapsed lobe. A hyperresonant note suggests pneumothorax or emphysema. A raised lower border may mean volume loss, effusion or an elevated hemidiaphragm.

A negative finding

Resonant and symmetric notes make a large effusion or lobar consolidation unlikely. They do not exclude a small effusion, a central lesion or an early pneumonia.

Evidence & limitations

Percussion is more useful for detecting a pleural effusion than a pneumonia. In pooled studies a dull percussion note raises the probability of pleural effusion appreciably (positive LR of roughly 5 to 8) while a normal percussion note makes a large effusion less likely; for pneumonia, dullness is only modestly helpful and the absence of it does not exclude pneumonia. Ultrasonography and chest radiography are more accurate.

Points to remember

  1. Keep the pleximeter finger flat on the skin and lift the hitting finger straight away, or the note is muffled.
  2. Do not percuss over the scapula or a rib; tap in an intercostal space.
  3. Always compare left with right at the same level, and use the sound you hear and the feel under your finger.
