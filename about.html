Bedside English
About

Who makes this, and how

Last updated 2026-08-28 · Developer: boyskier · Contact: bedsideenglish.support@gmail.com

Short version: Bedside English is a free, open-source project run by one developer. It publishes two things: an app for practicing spoken English with AI patients, and a library of worked examples — model histories, oral case presentations, SBAR and I-PASS scripts, and history-taking question guides.

Every published guide is built from a reviewed manifest, cites primary or authoritative sources, carries a visible review date, and uses fictional patients. Nothing on this site is medical advice, and nothing here claims to be a clinical authority.

1. What Bedside English is

Bedside English: Talk & Train is a personal, open-source project distributed free of charge — on GitHub for Windows, macOS, and Linux, and on Google Play for Android. There are no accounts, no subscription, and no advertising. The app runs on an AI provider key that you create and control.

It is an English-speaking practice tool for international medical graduates, medical students, and healthcare professionals working in English. It is not a clinical system, a medical device, a diagnostic service, or a substitute for professional medical advice, and it is not affiliated with OET, OSCE, USMLE, or any residency-matching organization.

The clinical checklists inside the app exist to give a conversation a shape so you have something to speak about. They are not teaching material for medicine and must not be used to support a real clinical decision.

2. Who writes it

This is a one-person open-source project. The developer builds the apps, writes and edits the site, and performs the review steps described below. The site does not claim a clinical or teaching credential, and no page carries a specialist byline. Where a clinical or communication-framework claim is made, the page links the primary source instead of asking you to trust an unnamed reviewer.

If something here is wrong, the fastest way to change it is to tell us — see corrections. Every page's full edit history is public in the site repository.

3. How a guide gets published

Guides are not written page by page. Each library — history-taking questions, model interviews, case presentations, team communication, everyday English — is generated by a script from a reviewed JSON manifest. The manifest is the publication allowlist: an entry that has not passed review is not in it, so it cannot be published by accident.

  1. Search task. Define the one question the page answers and who is asking it.
  2. Source pack. Collect at least two primary or authoritative sources — the framework's own owner, a public health or patient-safety agency, or a professional standards body. Secondary articles may help with discovery but are not used as the evidence base for a clinical communication claim.
  3. Fictional case facts. Build a chart that is internally consistent in its numbers and timings. No real patient data is ever used.
  4. Draft. Write the script, the wording explanations, the checklist, and the FAQ against those facts and sources.
  5. Review. Check the language, the sources, and the boundary where local policy takes over.
  6. Publish and date. Generate the page, stamp the review date on it, and add the URL to the sitemap.

The review gate

Each entry carries five attestations. All five must be true before the generator will emit a page:

AttestationWhat was checked
source_claims_checkedEvery framework and safety claim was checked against the listed sources.
fictional_case_labeledThe case is visibly and consistently labeled fictional.
local_policy_boundary_checkedThe page says where local protocol, supervision, or institutional policy takes over.
plain_language_checkedThe English is natural, current US usage that a learner can actually say aloud.
search_intent_checkedThe page answers the question it is written for, near the top.

Automated checks additionally refuse to build a page with an unknown field, an invalid slug, date or URL, a malformed framework step order, a duplicate fact ID, a required fact that is never spoken aloud, too few sources or FAQs, or an absolute safety claim.

What automation cannot do is judge clinical appropriateness, institution-specific escalation, realistic scope of practice, or a subtle contradiction between two paraphrases. Those remain human judgments, and they are the reason the attestations are blocking rather than advisory.

4. Where AI is and is not involved

  • The app is an AI product. The patient you talk to, the feedback, and the pronunciation scoring are generated live by the AI provider you configure with your own key. That output is not reviewed by anyone in advance, can be wrong, and should be treated as language practice rather than clinical instruction.
  • Site pages are static. No page on this website calls a live AI. Everything you read here was fixed at build time.
  • The two-voice model-interview audio is synthesized with Google's Gemini text-to-speech from the reviewed transcript, then stored as audio files. It is not a recording of real clinicians or real patients, and the page it sits on says so.
  • AI tooling assists drafting and code. It does not authorise publication: the review gate in section 3 stands between a draft and a live URL.

5. Sources, dates, and refreshes

Every guide lists its sources with links, and shows the date it was last reviewed. A published guide is re-reviewed at least every six months, and sooner when a source changes, a framework is revised, a product claim stops being accurate, or reader questions reveal a task the page does not actually answer. Pages that duplicate each other are merged or redirected rather than left to compete.

6. Corrections

Report an error — clinical, linguistic, or factual — in whichever way is easiest:

  • Email bedsideenglish.support@gmail.com with the page URL and the sentence in question.
  • Open an issue on the website repository.
  • Inside the Android app, use Report on an AI response to flag harmful, unsafe, misleading, or incorrect AI-generated content. What that sends is described in the privacy policy, §4.2.

Corrections to a published guide update the page's review date, and the change stays visible in the repository history.
