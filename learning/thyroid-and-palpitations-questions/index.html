How to ask about palpitations and thyroid symptoms in English

Practice natural American English questions to explore palpitations, unintentional weight changes, and heat sensitivity without medical jargon.

Quick answer

What can I ask in this scenario?

Ask whether the heart races at rest, if they have lost weight despite eating normally, whether they feel hot when others are comfortable, if they have noticed eye irritation or prominence, and check for family thyroid history.

“Does your heart ever feel like it is beating unusually fast, fluttering, or pounding, even when you are resting?”

Communication context

A realistic patient scenario

Imagine this consultation: A patient presents for a medical evaluation due to persistent heart racing, unexplained weight loss, and feeling unusually warm. Your task is to explore the history in natural English without overwhelming the patient with clinical jargon.

Clinical area
Endocrinology
Your focus
Clear, patient-friendly history taking

The clinical detail gives the language a realistic setting. This guide teaches communication, not diagnosis or treatment.

Useful questions for this scenario

Natural English questions, grouped by purpose

These are selected questions for this particular practice case. They are not an exhaustive clinical checklist.

The symptom story

“Have you noticed any changes in your eyes, such as feeling gritty, dry, or looking more prominent?”

Purpose: Screen for thyroid-related eye symptoms including gritty sensation or proptosis.

Why this wording

Phrasing as “feeling gritty or looking more prominent” is much more sensitive and conversational than using technical terms like exophthalmos.

Associated symptoms

“Does your heart ever feel like it is beating unusually fast, fluttering, or pounding, even when you are resting?”

Purpose: Assess palpitations, resting tachycardia, and irregular heart rhythms.

Why this wording

Giving everyday descriptors such as “racing, fluttering, or pounding” helps patients articulate heart sensations accurately.

Clinical term
“Have you noticed any palpitations or a rapid heartbeat?”
Patient-friendly
“Do you ever feel your heart pounding in your chest when you are sitting quietly?”

“Have you noticed any change in your weight recently, even if your appetite has stayed the same or increased?”

Purpose: Screen for unintentional weight loss alongside changes in appetite.

Why this wording

Asking about weight change and appetite together highlights the classic metabolic mismatch without assuming an exact number of pounds lost.

“Do you find yourself feeling uncomfortably hot or sweating more than usual, even in a cool room?”

Purpose: Evaluate temperature sensitivity and excessive perspiration.

Health background

“Does anyone in your family have a history of thyroid conditions or other autoimmune disorders?”

Purpose: Ask about hereditary thyroid and autoimmune disease.

Put the phrases together

Move from open to focused questions

  1. Open

    “Could you tell me more about what has been happening?”

  2. Focus

    Choose a relevant question from the groups above.

  3. Check

    “Is there anything else about this that you think I should know?”

Language note: Short, direct questions are often easier to answer. Ask one idea at a time, then follow the patient's wording when you need more detail.
