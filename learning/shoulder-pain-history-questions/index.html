How to ask about shoulder pain when reaching overhead in English

Use clear, patient-friendly questions to explore shoulder pain that's worse with overhead movement. Learn why each phrase works, then say it aloud.

Quick answer

What can I ask in this scenario?

Ask what movements bring on the pain, how long it's been going on, whether there's any weakness, whether it disturbs sleep, and explain that physical therapy is usually the first step.

“What activities specifically bring on the shoulder pain?”

Communication context

A realistic patient scenario

Imagine this consultation: A patient describes aching shoulder pain that's worse when reaching overhead. Your task is to explore the history in natural English without overwhelming the patient with clinical jargon.

Useful questions for this scenario

Natural English questions, grouped by purpose

These are selected questions for this particular practice case. They are not an exhaustive clinical checklist.

Associated symptoms

“What activities specifically bring on the shoulder pain?”

Purpose: Ask what specific movements or activities bring on the shoulder pain.

Why this wording

Asking for specific activities, rather than ‘when does it hurt,’ gives you concrete triggers, like reaching overhead or reaching behind the back, that help distinguish this pattern from other shoulder problems.

“How long has this been going on, and is it getting worse?”

Purpose: Ask how long the pain has been present and whether it's getting worse.

“Do you feel any actual weakness in the shoulder, or is it mainly pain that limits you?”

Purpose: Ask whether there's any true weakness, not just pain, which would need further evaluation.

Why this wording

Separating ‘weakness’ from ‘pain that limits movement’ matters clinically — pain alone usually responds well to physical therapy, while true weakness can point toward a tear that needs a closer look.

“Does the pain ever wake you up at night, especially if you roll onto that shoulder?”

Purpose: Ask whether the shoulder pain disturbs sleep, especially lying on that side.

Why this wording

Night pain that wakes a patient, especially with pressure on the shoulder, is one of the most useful details in a shoulder history, so it's worth asking as its own specific question.

The patient's perspective

“If examination supports tendinopathy, we can start physical therapy and discuss pain medicine after reviewing whether it is safe for you.”

Purpose: Explain that physical therapy is usually the first step, with pain medicine discussed after the exam if it's needed.

Put the phrases together

Move from open to focused questions

  1. Open

    “Could you tell me more about what has been happening?”

  2. Focus

    Choose a relevant question from the groups above.

  3. Check

    “Is there anything else about this that you think I should know?”

Language note: Short, direct questions are often easier to answer. Ask one idea at a time, then follow the patient's wording when you need more detail.
