How to ask about a child's cough and stridor in English

Learn how to communicate calmly with worried parents, ask about barking cough and noisy breathing, and explain next steps in clear, supportive English.

Quick answer

What can I ask in this scenario?

Ask the parent what the cough sounds like, whether they hear a harsh, high-pitched noise when the child breathes in at rest, if breathing worsens when lying flat, check for drooling or swallowing trouble, and reassure the family.

“How would you describe his cough? Does it sound harsh or barking, like a seal?”

Communication context

A realistic patient scenario

Imagine this consultation: A parent brings a young toddler to urgent care in the middle of the night because of a sudden harsh cough and noisy breathing. Your task is to explore the history in natural English without overwhelming the patient with clinical jargon.

Clinical area
Pediatrics
Your focus
Clear, patient-friendly history taking

The clinical detail gives the language a realistic setting. This guide teaches communication, not diagnosis or treatment.

Useful questions for this scenario

Natural English questions, grouped by purpose

These are selected questions for this particular practice case. They are not an exhaustive clinical checklist.

Associated symptoms

“How would you describe his cough? Does it sound harsh or barking, like a seal?”

Purpose: Help the parent describe what the child's cough sounds like.

Why this wording

Comparing the sound to a seal or dog bark gives parents a clear, memorable reference point for croup cough without using clinical jargon.

Open description
“What does his cough sound like to you?”
Focused check
“Has the cough sounded unusually deep or barky?”

“Have you heard a harsh, high-pitched noise when he breathes in, especially when he is resting quietly?”

Purpose: Ask if the parent hears a harsh, high-pitched noise when the child breathes in at rest.

Why this wording

Describing stridor as a “harsh, high-pitched noise” when breathing in helps parents distinguish it clearly from the expiratory whistling of a wheeze.

Sound check
“Does his breathing sound noisy or harsh when he takes a breath in?”

“Does his breathing seem harder or noisier when he lies down or gets upset?”

Purpose: Ask if the breathing difficulty gets worse when the child lies down or cries.

“Is he able to swallow liquids normally, or have you noticed any unusual drooling?”

Purpose: Check if the child is swallowing liquids normally or having unusual drooling.

The patient's perspective

“We’ll keep a close eye on his breathing and make sure he’s breathing comfortably.”

Purpose: Reassure the parent and explain that the clinical team will monitor the child's breathing closely.

Why this wording

Calmly explaining that the team is closely monitoring the child's breathing reassures anxious parents without making rigid treatment claims.

Reassuring explanation
“We'll keep a close eye on his breathing right now to make sure he stays comfortable.”

Put the phrases together

Move from open to focused questions

  1. Open

    “Could you tell me more about what has been happening?”

  2. Focus

    Choose a relevant question from the groups above.

  3. Check

    “Is there anything else about this that you think I should know?”

Language note: Short, direct questions are often easier to answer. Ask one idea at a time, then follow the patient's wording when you need more detail.
