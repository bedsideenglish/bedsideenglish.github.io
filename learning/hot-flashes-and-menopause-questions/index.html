How to ask about hot flashes and menopause symptoms in English

Use clear, patient-friendly questions to explore hot flashes and other menopause symptoms with sensitivity. Learn why each phrase works, then say it aloud.

Quick answer

What can I ask in this scenario?

Ask how often the hot flashes happen and how severe they are, whether night sweats disrupt sleep, when the last period was, whether there's any vaginal discomfort, and what concerns the patient about hormone therapy.

“How many hot flashes do you have per day, and how severe are they?”

Communication context

A realistic patient scenario

Imagine this consultation: A patient in her late fifties describes hot flashes, night sweats, and disrupted sleep, with no period for over a year. Your task is to explore the history in natural English without overwhelming the patient with clinical jargon.

Clinical area
Family Medicine

The clinical detail gives the language a realistic setting. This guide teaches communication, not diagnosis or treatment.

Useful questions for this scenario

Natural English questions, grouped by purpose

These are selected questions for this particular practice case. They are not an exhaustive clinical checklist.

The symptom story

“How many hot flashes do you have per day, and how severe are they?”

Purpose: Get a sense of how often the hot flashes happen and how much they affect the patient.

Why this wording

Asking for a rough number per day, rather than just ‘do you get hot flashes,’ gives you a baseline you can compare against after any treatment.

“How many months has it been since your last period?”

Purpose: Ask how many months it has been since the last period.

Associated symptoms

“Are the night sweats bad enough to wake you up, or to need a change of clothes or sheets?”

Purpose: Ask whether night sweats are severe enough to wake the patient or require changing clothes.

Why this wording

Using a concrete marker of severity — waking up, changing clothes — is easier to answer accurately than a vague ‘how bad are they,’ and it helps distinguish mild from disruptive symptoms.

“Have you had any vaginal dryness or discomfort during intimacy?”

Purpose: Ask about vaginal dryness or discomfort, which is common but often not mentioned unless asked directly.

Why this wording

This is a topic patients rarely bring up on their own, so asking directly — in plain, respectful language — is often the only way it gets addressed.

The patient's perspective

“What have you heard about hormone therapy that worries you?”

Purpose: Find out what the patient has heard about hormone therapy that worries them.

Put the phrases together

Move from open to focused questions

  1. Open

    “Could you tell me more about what has been happening?”

  2. Focus

    Choose a relevant question from the groups above.

  3. Check

    “Is there anything else about this that you think I should know?”

Language note: Short, direct questions are often easier to answer. Ask one idea at a time, then follow the patient's wording when you need more detail.
