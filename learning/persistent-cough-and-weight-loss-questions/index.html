How to ask about a persistent cough and weight loss in English

Use clear, respectful questions to explore a persistent cough with weight loss and night sweats. Learn why each phrase works, then say it aloud.

Quick answer

What can I ask in this scenario?

Ask how long the cough has lasted and whether there's any blood in it, whether night sweats soak through clothes or sheets, whether weight loss has been unintended, about travel or close-contact history, and whether they've ever coughed up a large amount of blood.

“How long have you had the cough, and have you coughed up any blood?”

Communication context

A realistic patient scenario

Imagine this consultation: A patient describes weeks of cough, night sweats, and weight loss without an obvious cause. Your task is to explore the history in natural English without overwhelming the patient with clinical jargon.

Useful questions for this scenario

Natural English questions, grouped by purpose

These are selected questions for this particular practice case. They are not an exhaustive clinical checklist.

The symptom story

“How long have you had the cough, and have you coughed up any blood?”

Purpose: Ask how long the cough has lasted and whether there has been any blood in it.

Associated symptoms

“Have you had night sweats bad enough to soak through your clothes or the sheets?”

Purpose: Ask about drenching night sweats that soak through clothes or sheets.

Why this wording

Anchoring the question to a concrete sign — soaking through clothes or sheets — separates ordinary night warmth from the more significant drenching sweats this question is meant to catch.

“Have you lost weight without trying?”

Purpose: Ask whether the patient has lost weight without trying.

Other useful questions

“Have you spent time somewhere TB is more common, or been in close contact with anyone who has it?”

Purpose: Ask about travel to, or time spent in, an area where tuberculosis is more common, or close contact with someone who has it.

Why this wording

Phrasing this as travel and contact history — rather than asking about the patient's own background or nationality — keeps the question focused on relevant exposure, not on the patient personally.

“Have you ever coughed up a large amount of blood at once?”

Purpose: Screen for a large amount of coughed-up blood, which needs urgent attention.

Why this wording

This is asked as its own question, separate from the general ‘blood in the cough’ question above, because a large volume changes the urgency of the visit entirely.

Put the phrases together

Move from open to focused questions

  1. Open

    “Could you tell me more about what has been happening?”

  2. Focus

    Choose a relevant question from the groups above.

  3. Check

    “Is there anything else about this that you think I should know?”

Language note: Short, direct questions are often easier to answer. Ask one idea at a time, then follow the patient's wording when you need more detail.
