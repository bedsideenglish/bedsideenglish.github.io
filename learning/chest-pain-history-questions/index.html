How to ask about chest pain in English

Use clear, patient-friendly questions to explore a history of chest pain for 2 hours. Learn what each phrase is trying to clarify, then say it aloud.

Quick answer

What can I ask in this scenario?

Start with an open invitation, then use focused questions about site and radiation of the pain, onset and duration, character of the pain, and associated autonomic symptoms. The phrases below are selected for this scenario, so adapt them to the patient's answers.

“Where exactly is the pain, and does it move anywhere else?”

Communication context

A realistic patient scenario

Imagine this consultation: A patient reports chest pain for 2 hours. Your task is to explore the history in natural English without overwhelming the patient with clinical jargon.

Useful questions for this scenario

Natural English questions, grouped by purpose

These are selected questions for this particular practice case. They are not an exhaustive clinical checklist.

The symptom story

“Where exactly is the pain, and does it move anywhere else?”

Purpose: Site and radiation of the pain.

“When did it start, and how long does it last each time?”

Purpose: Onset and duration.

“How would you describe it — sharp, crushing, burning, tight?”

Purpose: Character of the pain.

Associated symptoms

“Along with the pain, did you feel sweaty, sick, or short of breath?”

Purpose: Associated autonomic symptoms.

Health background

“Do you have high blood pressure, diabetes, or high cholesterol, and do you smoke?”

Purpose: Cardiac risk factors.

“Has anyone in your family had a heart attack, especially young?”

Purpose: Family history of early heart disease.

The patient's perspective

“What are you most worried this might be?”

Purpose: The patient's own worry.

Put the phrases together

Move from open to focused questions

  1. Open

    “Could you tell me more about what has been happening?”

  2. Focus

    Choose a relevant question from the groups above.

  3. Check

    “Is there anything else about this that you think I should know?”

Language note: A plain expression such as “Does it move anywhere else?” can be easier for a patient to understand than a technical term such as “Does it radiate?”
