How to ask about sudden flank pain in English

Use clear, patient-friendly questions to explore sudden, severe flank pain. Learn why each phrase works, then say it aloud.

Quick answer

What can I ask in this scenario?

Ask about the pattern and spread of the pain, whether the urine looks pink or tea-colored, whether nausea or vomiting comes with the pain, about a personal or family history of kidney stones, and screen for fever, chills, or confusion.

“Does the pain come in severe waves, and does it spread down toward your groin?”

Communication context

A realistic patient scenario

Imagine this consultation: A patient arrives with sudden, severe pain in the side that comes in waves and spreads toward the groin. Your task is to explore the history in natural English without overwhelming the patient with clinical jargon.

Useful questions for this scenario

Natural English questions, grouped by purpose

These are selected questions for this particular practice case. They are not an exhaustive clinical checklist.

The symptom story

“Does the pain come in severe waves, and does it spread down toward your groin?”

Purpose: Find out whether the pain comes in severe waves and spreads toward the groin.

Why this wording

The base question uses ‘groin’ as a comfortable general term for a first pass; the more anatomically specific alternative is only needed if the patient's answer is unclear, since it's more clinical and more personal to ask outright.

If more detail is needed
“Does the pain spread down into your groin, or into a testicle or labia?”

Associated symptoms

“Have you noticed pink, red, or tea-colored urine when you go to the bathroom?”

Purpose: Ask whether the patient has noticed pink, red, or tea-colored urine.

Why this wording

Listing several possible colors, not just ‘blood in your urine,’ helps patients recognize a subtler change they might not have labeled as ‘blood.’

“Do you feel extremely nauseous or vomit when the pain peaks?”

Purpose: Ask whether nausea or vomiting comes with the pain, especially when it peaks.

“Do you have a fever, shaking chills, or any confusion right now?”

Purpose: Screen for fever, shaking chills, or confusion, which would point to a more urgent infected obstruction.

Why this wording

This is asked even though the visit is about pain, because fever or confusion alongside a kidney stone changes this from a pain-management case into a same-day emergency — ‘right now’ keeps the focus on current, not past, symptoms.

Health background

“Have you or anyone in your family passed a kidney stone in the past?”

Purpose: Ask whether the patient or a family member has passed a kidney stone before.

Put the phrases together

Move from open to focused questions

  1. Open

    “Could you tell me more about what has been happening?”

  2. Focus

    Choose a relevant question from the groups above.

  3. Check

    “Is there anything else about this that you think I should know?”

Language note: A plain expression such as “Does it move anywhere else?” can be easier for a patient to understand than a technical term such as “Does it radiate?”
