How to take a headache history in English

Master essential American English questions for evaluating headaches, exploring associated symptoms like nausea and light sensitivity, and reassuring patients.

Quick answer

What can I ask in this scenario?

Begin by asking where the pain is and how it feels, then ask about nausea, light or sound sensitivity, how long the episodes last, whether resting in a dark room helps, and what worries the patient most.

“Where do you feel the headache, and what does the pain feel like?”

Communication context

A realistic patient scenario

Imagine this consultation: A patient attends the clinic seeking help for recurrent headaches that are accompanied by nausea. Your task is to explore the history in natural English without overwhelming the patient with clinical jargon.

Useful questions for this scenario

Natural English questions, grouped by purpose

These are selected questions for this particular practice case. They are not an exhaustive clinical checklist.

The symptom story

“Where do you feel the headache, and what does the pain feel like?”

Purpose: Explore where the headache is located and whether it feels throbbing, dull, or sharp.

Why this wording

Starting with an open question avoids leading the patient to say the pain is only one-sided before they describe it in their own words.

Direct follow-up
“Is the pain mostly on one side of your head, or on both sides?”
Descriptive options
“Would you describe the pain as throbbing, tight, or steady pressure?”

“How often do you get these headaches, and how long does a typical one last?”

Purpose: Determine the frequency and typical duration of headache episodes.

“Does resting in a quiet, dark room help relieve the pain?”

Purpose: Ask what measures or environments make the headache better.

Why this wording

Asking about a quiet, dark room provides a specific, practical example of a non-medication relieving factor.

Associated symptoms

“When you have a headache, do you feel nauseated, or do bright lights and loud sounds bother you?”

Purpose: Screen for nausea, light sensitivity, and sound sensitivity.

Why this wording

Using “bother you” offers a natural, conversational way to check for photophobia and phonophobia without using complex terminology.

The patient's perspective

“Is there anything in particular you are most concerned about regarding these headaches?”

Purpose: Understand the patient's underlying worries and expectations.

Put the phrases together

Move from open to focused questions

  1. Open

    “Could you tell me more about what has been happening?”

  2. Focus

    Choose a relevant question from the groups above.

  3. Check

    “Is there anything else about this that you think I should know?”

Language note: Short, direct questions are often easier to answer. Ask one idea at a time, then follow the patient's wording when you need more detail.
