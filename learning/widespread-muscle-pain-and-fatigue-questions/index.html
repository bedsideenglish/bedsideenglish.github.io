How to ask about widespread muscle pain and fatigue in English

Use clear, patient-friendly questions to explore widespread pain and fatigue. Learn why each phrase works, then say it aloud.

Quick answer

What can I ask in this scenario?

Ask whether the pain is widespread or in specific joints, whether sleep feels restorative, whether there's trouble concentrating, how long this has been going on, and about related conditions like irritable bowel syndrome.

“Is the pain all over your body, or in specific joints?”

Communication context

A realistic patient scenario

Imagine this consultation: A patient describes a year of widespread muscle pain, poor sleep, and trouble concentrating. Your task is to explore the history in natural English without overwhelming the patient with clinical jargon.

Useful questions for this scenario

Natural English questions, grouped by purpose

These are selected questions for this particular practice case. They are not an exhaustive clinical checklist.

The symptom story

“Is the pain all over your body, or in specific joints?”

Purpose: Ask whether the pain is widespread across the body or limited to specific joints.

Why this wording

This distinction — widespread muscle pain versus pain in specific joints — is one of the most useful details in this history, since it points toward very different next steps, so it's worth asking clearly and early.

“How long has this been going on?”

Purpose: Ask how long this has been going on.

Associated symptoms

“When you wake up in the morning, do you feel rested, or still exhausted?”

Purpose: Ask whether the patient wakes up feeling rested or still exhausted.

Why this wording

Framing this around how mornings feel, rather than asking simply ‘do you sleep well,’ captures unrefreshing sleep even in a patient who technically sleeps a full night.

“Have you had trouble concentrating, or 'brain fog'?”

Purpose: Ask about trouble concentrating or ‘brain fog’.

Why this wording

Using the patient's own likely term, ‘brain fog,’ alongside the more formal ‘trouble concentrating’ gives the patient an easy way to recognize and name a symptom that can otherwise feel hard to describe.

Health background

“Do you have irritable bowel syndrome or other chronic symptoms?”

Purpose: Ask about related conditions, like irritable bowel syndrome, that often occur together with this pattern.

Put the phrases together

Move from open to focused questions

  1. Open

    “Could you tell me more about what has been happening?”

  2. Focus

    Choose a relevant question from the groups above.

  3. Check

    “Is there anything else about this that you think I should know?”

Language note: Short, direct questions are often easier to answer. Ask one idea at a time, then follow the patient's wording when you need more detail.
