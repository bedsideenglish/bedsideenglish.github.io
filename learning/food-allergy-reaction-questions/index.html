How to ask about a food allergy reaction in English

Use clear, calm questions to find out what happened and how serious the reaction is. Learn why each phrase works, then say it aloud.

Quick answer

What can I ask in this scenario?

Start by asking what they ate and how quickly symptoms began, then check for airway symptoms like throat tightness, look for hives or swelling, ask about a known allergy history, and find out whether they used an epinephrine auto-injector.

“What did you eat, and how soon after did the symptoms start?”

Communication context

A realistic patient scenario

Imagine this consultation: A patient comes in with a suspected allergic reaction after eating. Your task is to explore the history in natural English without overwhelming the patient with clinical jargon.

Useful questions for this scenario

Natural English questions, grouped by purpose

These are selected questions for this particular practice case. They are not an exhaustive clinical checklist.

The symptom story

“What did you eat, and how soon after did the symptoms start?”

Purpose: Find out what was eaten and how quickly the symptoms started.

“Do you have hives, or swelling of your lips or tongue?”

Purpose: Look for hives and swelling of the lips, tongue, or face.

Why this wording

‘Swelling of your lips or tongue’ is easier for a patient to picture than the equivalent medical term, and naming specific body parts helps a frightened patient answer quickly.

Associated symptoms

“Are you having any trouble breathing, or does your throat feel tight?”

Purpose: Check for the most dangerous symptoms first — breathing and airway involvement.

Why this wording

Leading with the breathing question puts the highest-risk symptom first, matching how a rapid allergy assessment is actually prioritized.

Very direct
“Can you breathe okay right now?”
Softer phrasing
“Are you having any trouble breathing, or does your throat feel tight?”

Health background

“Have you ever been told you're allergic to peanuts, or is this the first time this has happened?”

Purpose: Find out if this is a known allergy or a first-time reaction.

Why this wording

Offering ‘first time’ as an explicit option avoids assuming the patient already knows they have a diagnosed allergy.

“Do you carry an epinephrine auto-injector, and have you used it?”

Purpose: Ask whether they carry rescue medication and whether they've already used it.

Why this wording

Asking whether it was used, not just whether they carry one, tells you if the reaction is already being treated or still needs immediate epinephrine.

Put the phrases together

Move from open to focused questions

  1. Open

    “Could you tell me more about what has been happening?”

  2. Focus

    Choose a relevant question from the groups above.

  3. Check

    “Is there anything else about this that you think I should know?”

Language note: Short, direct questions are often easier to answer. Ask one idea at a time, then follow the patient's wording when you need more detail.
