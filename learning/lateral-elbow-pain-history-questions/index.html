How to ask about lateral elbow pain in English

Use clear, patient-friendly questions to explore outer elbow pain linked to gripping and repetitive wrist motion. Learn why each phrase works, then say it aloud.

Quick answer

What can I ask in this scenario?

Ask exactly where the pain is and whether gripping makes it worse, how much repetitive wrist motion the job involves, how long it's been going on and what's been tried, whether there's any neck pain or arm numbness, and explain the usual bracing and rehab plan.

“Can you point exactly where it hurts, and does gripping tools make it worse?”

Communication context

A realistic patient scenario

Imagine this consultation: A plumber describes chronic pain over the outer elbow that's worse when gripping tools at work. Your task is to explore the history in natural English without overwhelming the patient with clinical jargon.

Useful questions for this scenario

Natural English questions, grouped by purpose

These are selected questions for this particular practice case. They are not an exhaustive clinical checklist.

Associated symptoms

“Can you point exactly where it hurts, and does gripping tools make it worse?”

Purpose: Ask the patient to point to exactly where it hurts and confirm that gripping makes it worse.

Why this wording

Asking the patient to point to the spot, not just describe it, is often faster and more precise than a verbal description alone for a localized pain like this.

“How long has this been going on, and have you tried anything for it?”

Purpose: Ask how long this has been going on and what's already been tried.

“Do you have any neck pain or numbness going down your arm?”

Purpose: Ask about neck pain or numbness traveling down the arm, which would point to a different cause.

Why this wording

This question is included even though the pain seems clearly localized to the elbow, because neck problems can sometimes cause pain that's felt at the elbow — asking it directly helps rule out a different source before assuming the elbow itself is the problem.

The patient's perspective

“We recommend a counterforce brace, stretching exercises, and modifying your grip technique at work.”

Purpose: Explain the usual plan — a counterforce brace, stretching, and adjusting grip technique at work.

Daily life and context

“How much repetitive gripping or wrist motion does your plumbing work require?”

Purpose: Ask how much repetitive gripping or wrist motion the patient's work involves.

Why this wording

Naming the patient's specific trade, rather than asking a generic ‘is your job physical,’ shows you're listening to their situation and usually gets a much more detailed, useful answer.

Put the phrases together

Move from open to focused questions

  1. Open

    “Could you tell me more about what has been happening?”

  2. Focus

    Choose a relevant question from the groups above.

  3. Check

    “Is there anything else about this that you think I should know?”

Language note: Short, direct questions are often easier to answer. Ask one idea at a time, then follow the patient's wording when you need more detail.
