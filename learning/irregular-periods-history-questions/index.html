How to ask about irregular periods in English

Use clear, patient-friendly questions to explore irregular periods without assuming what the patient wants or has noticed. Learn why each phrase works, then say it aloud.

Quick answer

What can I ask in this scenario?

Ask how the cycle pattern compares to before, whether there's been increased hair growth or acne, whether weight has changed, whether the patient is trying to become pregnant, and whether there are any symptoms suggesting a thyroid problem.

“How often do you get your period now compared to before?”

Communication context

A realistic patient scenario

Imagine this consultation: A patient describes irregular periods along with increased hair growth and acne. Your task is to explore the history in natural English without overwhelming the patient with clinical jargon.

Clinical area
Obstetrics & Gynecology
Your focus
Clear, patient-friendly history taking

The clinical detail gives the language a realistic setting. This guide teaches communication, not diagnosis or treatment.

Useful questions for this scenario

Natural English questions, grouped by purpose

These are selected questions for this particular practice case. They are not an exhaustive clinical checklist.

Associated symptoms

“How often do you get your period now compared to before?”

Purpose: Ask how often periods happen now compared to before.

“Have you noticed any new hair growth on your face or body, or any new acne?”

Purpose: Ask about increased hair growth on the face or body, or new acne.

Why this wording

Adding ‘new’ to both hair growth and acne makes clear you're asking about a change, not judging the patient's normal appearance — an important distinction for a topic that can otherwise feel sensitive.

“Has your weight changed recently, especially around your abdomen?”

Purpose: Ask whether weight has changed recently, especially around the abdomen.

“Have you had any symptoms of thyroid problems, like cold intolerance or fatigue?”

Purpose: Screen for symptoms of a thyroid problem, like cold intolerance or fatigue, since these can also cause irregular periods.

The patient's perspective

“Are you currently trying to become pregnant, or thinking about it?”

“If so, how long have you been trying?”

Purpose: Find out whether the patient is currently trying to become pregnant before asking how long.

Why this wording

The source case wording assumed the patient was already trying to conceive by asking ‘how long have you been trying.’ Asking first whether they're trying at all avoids that assumption — some patients with irregular periods aren't pursuing pregnancy, and assuming otherwise can feel presumptuous or, for some patients, upsetting.

Put the phrases together

Move from open to focused questions

  1. Open

    “Could you tell me more about what has been happening?”

  2. Focus

    Choose a relevant question from the groups above.

  3. Check

    “Is there anything else about this that you think I should know?”

Language note: Short, direct questions are often easier to answer. Ask one idea at a time, then follow the patient's wording when you need more detail.
