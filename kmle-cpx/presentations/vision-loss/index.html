Prioritize the patient's condition. Sudden vision loss requires urgent assessment whether or not it is painful. With severe eye pain or new neurologic symptoms, do not rely on normal findings shown in a technique card or delay assessment to finish the examination checklist.

Vision loss: history-taking questions and sample wording

  1. 01

    Onset: sudden or gradually progressive

    What to say to the patient

    When did your vision change? Did it happen suddenly, or become blurry gradually?

  2. 02

    Unilateral or bilateral involvement, checked by covering each eye separately

    What to say to the patient

    Is it one eye or both? What happens when you cover each eye in turn?

  3. 03

    Transient visual loss with recovery, and its duration

    What to say to the patient

    Have you briefly lost vision and then had it return? About how many minutes did that last?

  4. 04

    Pattern of visual change: overall blurring, central loss, narrowed peripheral vision, or distorted straight lines

    What to say to the patient

    Is everything blurry, is the center of your vision missing, or is it harder to see off to the sides? Do straight lines look wavy?

  5. 05

    Whether near or distance vision is affected, and when the glasses prescription last changed

    What to say to the patient

    Is it harder to see things up close or far away? When did you last get new glasses?

  6. 06

    Light sensitivity, glare or halos, and difficulty driving at night

    What to say to the patient

    When you drive at night, do lights seem to spread out or cause glare?

  7. 07

    Floaters, flashing lights, and a curtain-like visual obstruction

    What to say to the patient

    Have you suddenly noticed many floaters or flashes of light? Does it look as though a curtain is blocking part of your vision?

  8. 08

    Diplopia and whether it resolves when one eye is covered

    What to say to the patient

    Do you see double? Does covering one eye make the double vision go away?

  9. 09

    Ptosis, worsening in the evening, and difficulty chewing or swallowing

    Select as clinically indicated

    What to say to the patient

    Have your eyelids been drooping? Is it worse in the evening? Have you had difficulty chewing or swallowing?

  10. 10

    Eye pain, pain with eye movement, and redness

    What to say to the patient

    Is your eye painful or red? Does it hurt to move it?

  11. 11

    New headache, temporal tenderness, jaw pain with chewing, and shoulder stiffness

    Applies to: adults

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had a headache recently, or tenderness when you press on your temple? Does your jaw hurt when you chew?

  12. 12

    Limb weakness or tingling, slurred speech, and dizziness

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had weakness or tingling in your arms or legs, or any slurred speech?

  13. 13

    Eye or head trauma

    What to say to the patient

    Have you recently hit or injured your eye or head?

  14. 14

    Duration and control of diabetes or hypertension, and previous fundus examination

    What to say to the patient

    Do you have diabetes or high blood pressure? When was your last retinal examination?

  15. 15

    Vascular risk factors, including atrial fibrillation and carotid disease

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been told you have an irregular heartbeat or narrowing of the arteries in your neck?

  16. 16

    High myopia, glaucoma diagnosis, and previous eye surgery, including cataract surgery

    What to say to the patient

    Are you very nearsighted, or have you been diagnosed with glaucoma? Have you had any eye surgery?

  17. 17

    Medications that may affect vision, including corticosteroids, hydroxychloroquine, and ethambutol

    Select as clinically indicated

    What to say to the patient

    Have you taken steroids, medication for a rheumatic condition, or tuberculosis medication?

  18. 18

    Family history of glaucoma or macular degeneration

    What to say to the patient

    Has anyone in your family had glaucoma or macular degeneration?

  19. 19

    Impact on driving, reading, walking, and other daily activities; falls

    What to say to the patient

    Has your vision made driving, reading, or walking difficult? Have you fallen because of it?

Which physical examinations should I consider?

These are the app's examination items for this clinical presentation. Use the available technique cards to review how to perform them. Specific examinations without a card remain in the checklist.

  1. 01

    Measure visual acuity in each eye separately and assess improvement with a pinhole

    • Visual acuity and pinhole testing, one eye at a time ↗
  2. 02

    Assess the pupillary light reflex and perform the swinging flashlight test for a relative afferent pupillary defect

    • Pupillary light reflex and swinging flashlight test (RAPD) ↗
  3. 03

    Confrontation visual field testing

    • Confrontation visual fields: four quadrants, one eye at a time ↗
  4. 04

    Use an Amsler grid to assess central visual distortion

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Technique card pending
  5. 05

    Assess eye movements and ptosis when diplopia is present

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Extraocular movements: six diagnostic positions of gaze ↗
  6. 06

    Use an ophthalmoscope to assess the red reflex and fundus, including the optic disc and retina

    • Direct ophthalmoscopy: approach and viewing the fundus ↗
  7. 07

    Palpate the temporal artery for tenderness and pulsation

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Temporal artery: branches, pulse, and tenderness ↗

Common consultation and examination flow

Expand the opening, general history, and pre- and post-examination items

Opening the consultation

  1. 01

    Greet the patient and confirm their name

    What to say to the patient

    Hello. Could you tell me your name?

  2. 02

    Introduce yourself as a medical student and ask for consent to proceed

    What to say to the patient

    My name is [name], and I'm a medical student. Is it okay if I talk with you about your health today?

  3. 03

    Help the patient feel at ease and ask them to tell you right away if they are uncomfortable

    What to say to the patient

    Please let me know right away if you're uncomfortable at any point.

Before taking the history

  1. 01

    Ask an open-ended question about the reason for the visit

    What to say to the patient

    What brings you in today?

  2. 02

    Check for other concerns and agree on the issues to address today

    What to say to the patient

    Is there anything else that's been bothering you?

After taking the history

  1. 01

    Summarize the main symptoms during the interview and confirm your understanding with the patient

    What to say to the patient

    Let me make sure I've understood. You came in because of [main concern], and you've been experiencing [summary]. Is that right?

  2. 02

    Ask about medical history, including diagnoses, hospitalizations, and surgery

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been diagnosed with or treated for any medical conditions? Have you ever had surgery?

  3. 03

    Ask about current prescription medications, over-the-counter medications, and supplements

    What to say to the patient

    Are you taking any medications or supplements?

  4. 04

    Ask about relevant social history, including alcohol, smoking, work, and lifestyle

    What to say to the patient

    Do you drink alcohol or smoke? What do you do for work?

  5. 05

    Ask about relevant family history

    What to say to the patient

    Does anyone in your family have a similar condition?

  6. 06

    Ask about the last menstrual period and the possibility of pregnancy

    Applies to: women of reproductive age

    What to say to the patient

    When did your last period start? Is there any chance you could be pregnant?

Before examination · when indicated

  1. 01

    Explain the area and method of examination and obtain consent

    What to say to the patient

    I'd like to examine you now. Is that okay?

  2. 02

    Clean your hands before the examination and tell the patient you are doing so

    What to say to the patient

    I'm going to clean my hands first.

  3. 03

    Protect privacy by exposing only the area being examined and keeping the rest covered

    What to say to the patient

    I'll only uncover the area I need to examine, and I'll cover you back up right afterward.

After examination · if performed

  1. 01

    Summarize and explain the examination findings to the patient

    What to say to the patient

    When I examined you, there was tenderness when I pressed on [area].
