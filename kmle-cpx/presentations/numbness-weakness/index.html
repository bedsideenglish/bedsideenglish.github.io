Prioritize the patient's condition. Sudden one-sided weakness, sensory changes, or speech changes, or rapidly progressive paralysis, are warning signs requiring urgent evaluation. Also check for severe respiratory muscle weakness or new bladder or bowel dysfunction. Do not delay evaluation to complete detailed provocative testing.

Weakness and sensory changes: history questions and examples

  1. 01

    Nature of symptoms: tingling or reduced sensation, true weakness, or both

    What to say to the patient

    Is it a tingling sensation, or is there actual weakness that makes it hard to move?

  2. 02

    Location and distribution: one-sided limb involvement, bilateral symmetry, specific fingers, or a stocking-glove pattern

    What to say to the patient

    Where do you notice it? Is it on one side or both? Which fingers are affected?

  3. 03

    Onset: sudden, over several days, or gradually over months; last known well time

    What to say to the patient

    When did this first start? Was it sudden? About what time were you last feeling normal?

  4. 04

    Course: spreading, worsening, improvement, or recurrence

    What to say to the patient

    Has it spread or gotten worse since it started? Or does it get better and worse?

  5. 05

    Functional difficulties: buttoning clothes, dropping objects, climbing stairs, or walking

    What to say to the patient

    Is it hard to button your clothes or hold things? How about climbing stairs or walking?

  6. 06

    Aggravating factors: nighttime worsening, wrist use, neck movement, or increasing fatigue later in the day

    What to say to the patient

    Is it worse at night or when you use your hands a lot? Does your strength fade toward the evening?

  7. 07

    Neck or low back pain radiating to an arm or leg

    What to say to the patient

    Do you have neck or low back pain that travels down an arm or leg?

  8. 08

    Slurred speech, facial droop, visual disturbance, dizziness, or difficulty swallowing

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had slurred speech or drooping on one side of your face? Is your vision okay?

  9. 09

    Bladder or bowel dysfunction; reduced perianal or saddle sensation

    What to say to the patient

    Have you developed any problems urinating or having bowel movements, or numbness around your buttocks?

  10. 10

    Shortness of breath; difficulty swallowing or chewing; drooping eyelids; double vision

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had shortness of breath or trouble swallowing food? Have your eyelids drooped, or have you had double vision?

  11. 11

    Muscle twitching or wasting

    What to say to the patient

    Have your muscles been twitching on their own, or do your hand muscles look smaller?

  12. 12

    Preceding infection, such as recent diarrhea or a cold

    What to say to the patient

    Did you have diarrhea or a cold a few weeks before these symptoms started?

  13. 13

    Vascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, and smoking

    What to say to the patient

    Do you have high blood pressure, diabetes, or high cholesterol? Have you been told you have an irregular heartbeat?

  14. 14

    Diabetes control; alcohol use; vitamin B12 deficiency risks, such as gastrectomy or a vegetarian diet

    What to say to the patient

    If you have diabetes, is it well controlled? Have you had stomach surgery, or do you drink alcohol often?

  15. 15

    Medications affecting muscles or nerves, such as diuretics, chemotherapy, or corticosteroids

    What to say to the patient

    Have you recently taken any medications, such as diuretics or chemotherapy?

  16. 16

    Repetitive wrist or elbow use, prolonged squatting, or leaning on the elbows

    What to say to the patient

    Does your work involve using your wrists a lot or keeping your elbows bent for a long time?

Which physical examinations should I consider?

These are the app's examination items for this clinical presentation. Use the available technique cards to review how to perform them. Specific examinations without a card remain in the checklist.

  1. 01

    Check blood pressure, pulse regularity, and blood glucose

    • Radial pulse: rate and rhythm ↗
    • Upper-arm blood pressure: standard measurement and comparison of both arms ↗
    • Capillary blood glucose · safe fingerstick sampling ↗
  2. 02

    Screen for stroke: facial symmetry, speech, arm drift, and visual fields

    • Pronator drift test ↗
    • Facial movements: comparison of the upper and lower face ↗
    • Confrontation visual fields: four quadrants, one eye at a time ↗
    • Observe speech articulation and language Technique card pending
  3. 03

    Compare strength on both sides and proximally versus distally; look for muscle atrophy and fasciculations

    • Pronator drift test ↗
    • Upper limb strength: shoulder and elbow ↗
    • Lower limb strength testing (nerve roots L2–S1) ↗
  4. 04

    Map sensory loss using light touch, pinprick, and vibration

    • Light touch sensation: comparison of both sides ↗
    • Great toe vibration and joint position sense ↗
    • Lower limb pinprick: key sensory points for L2–S1 ↗
  5. 05

    Deep tendon reflexes and Babinski sign

    • Patellar reflex (L2–L4, primarily L4) ↗
    • Plantar response: flexor and extensor (Babinski) ↗
    • Biceps reflex (C5–C6) ↗
    • Brachioradialis reflex (primarily C6) ↗
    • Triceps reflex (primarily C7) ↗
    • Achilles reflex (primarily S1) ↗
  6. 06

    If indicated, perform Phalen and Tinel tests, the Spurling test, and the straight leg raise

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Spurling test ↗
    • Phalen test: a gentle wrist flexion variation ↗
    • Tinel sign at the carpal tunnel ↗
    • Straight leg raise, including the Bragard maneuver ↗

Common consultation and examination flow

Expand the opening, general history, and pre- and post-examination items

Opening the consultation

  1. 01

    Greet the patient and confirm their name

    What to say to the patient

    Hello. Could you tell me your name?

  2. 02

    Introduce yourself as a medical student and ask for consent to proceed

    What to say to the patient

    My name is [name], and I'm a medical student. Is it okay if I talk with you about your health today?

  3. 03

    Help the patient feel at ease and ask them to tell you right away if they are uncomfortable

    What to say to the patient

    Please let me know right away if you're uncomfortable at any point.

Before taking the history

  1. 01

    Ask an open-ended question about the reason for the visit

    What to say to the patient

    What brings you in today?

  2. 02

    Check for other concerns and agree on the issues to address today

    What to say to the patient

    Is there anything else that's been bothering you?

After taking the history

  1. 01

    Summarize the main symptoms during the interview and confirm your understanding with the patient

    What to say to the patient

    Let me make sure I've understood. You came in because of [main concern], and you've been experiencing [summary]. Is that right?

  2. 02

    Ask about medical history, including diagnoses, hospitalizations, and surgery

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been diagnosed with or treated for any medical conditions? Have you ever had surgery?

  3. 03

    Ask about current prescription medications, over-the-counter medications, and supplements

    What to say to the patient

    Are you taking any medications or supplements?

  4. 04

    Ask about relevant social history, including alcohol, smoking, work, and lifestyle

    What to say to the patient

    Do you drink alcohol or smoke? What do you do for work?

  5. 05

    Ask about relevant family history

    What to say to the patient

    Does anyone in your family have a similar condition?

  6. 06

    Ask about the last menstrual period and the possibility of pregnancy

    Applies to: women of reproductive age

    What to say to the patient

    When did your last period start? Is there any chance you could be pregnant?

Before examination · when indicated

  1. 01

    Explain the area and method of examination and obtain consent

    What to say to the patient

    I'd like to examine you now. Is that okay?

  2. 02

    Clean your hands before the examination and tell the patient you are doing so

    What to say to the patient

    I'm going to clean my hands first.

  3. 03

    Protect privacy by exposing only the area being examined and keeping the rest covered

    What to say to the patient

    I'll only uncover the area I need to examine, and I'll cover you back up right afterward.

After examination · if performed

  1. 01

    Summarize and explain the examination findings to the patient

    What to say to the patient

    When I examined you, there was tenderness when I pressed on [area].
