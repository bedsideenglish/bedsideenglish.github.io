Prioritize the patient's condition. For findings suggesting cauda equina syndrome, such as saddle sensory loss, bowel or bladder dysfunction, or progressive leg weakness, or red flags such as fever or a history of cancer, avoid forceful spinal manipulation and proceed to immediate emergency assessment.

Neck and low back pain: history-taking questions and sample wording

  1. 01

    Pain location and radiation into the leg, including below the knee

    What to say to the patient

    Where in your lower back does it hurt? Does it spread into your buttock or leg? Does it go below your knee?

  2. 02

    Time of onset and precipitating event, including heavy lifting, a fall, or other trauma

    What to say to the patient

    When did the pain start? Did it start after lifting something heavy or after a fall?

  3. 03

    Pain quality and severity

    What to say to the patient

    What does the pain feel like: an ache, a stabbing pain, or an electric shock with tingling? How bad is it on a scale of 0 to 10?

  4. 04

    Aggravating and relieving factors: movement, position, coughing, and sneezing

    What to say to the patient

    What positions make it worse, and what makes it feel better? Does coughing or sneezing make it worse?

  5. 05

    Pain that persists while lying at rest or worsens at night

    What to say to the patient

    Does it keep hurting even when you're lying still, or get worse at night and wake you up?

  6. 06

    Morning stiffness lasting 30 minutes or longer and improvement with exercise

    What to say to the patient

    Is your back stiff for more than 30 minutes in the morning? Does moving around or exercising actually make it better?

  7. 07

    Leg tingling, reduced sensation, and weakness

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had tingling, numbness, or weakness in your legs?

  8. 08

    Reduced anal or perineal sensation and problems with bladder or bowel control

    What to say to the patient

    Have you noticed numbness around your anus, trouble passing urine or stool, or any leaking of urine or stool?

  9. 09

    Leg tingling with walking that improves with sitting or bending forward

    What to say to the patient

    When you walk, do your legs tingle or feel tight enough that you have to stop and rest? Does sitting down or bending forward help?

  10. 10

    Fever, unexplained weight loss, and sweating

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had a fever, unexplained weight loss, or night sweats recently?

  11. 11

    History of cancer

    What to say to the patient

    Have you ever been diagnosed with or treated for cancer?

  12. 12

    Recent infection, spinal procedures or injections, diabetes or immunosuppression, and injection drug use

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had an infection or any injections or procedures in your back recently? Do you have diabetes or a condition that weakens your immune system?

  13. 13

    Osteoporosis, long-term corticosteroid use, and minor falls

    What to say to the patient

    Do you have osteoporosis, or have you taken steroid medication for a long time? Did the pain start after even a minor fall?

  14. 14

    Urinary symptoms, hematuria, abdominal pain, and a pulsating sensation in the abdomen

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had discomfort when urinating, or blood in your urine? Any abdominal pain or a pulsing feeling in your abdomen?

  15. 15

    Red or painful eyes, heel pain, chronic diarrhea, and psoriasis

    What to say to the patient

    Do your eyes often get red or painful, do your heels hurt, or do you have frequent diarrhea?

  16. 16

    Analgesics taken and their effect

    What to say to the patient

    Have you tried any pain relievers? Did they help?

  17. 17

    Occupation, posture, stress, and mood as contributors to persistent pain

    What to say to the patient

    What position are you usually in at work? How have your stress level and mood been lately?

Which physical examinations should I consider?

These are the app's examination items for this clinical presentation. Use the available technique cards to review how to perform them. Specific examinations without a card remain in the checklist.

  1. 01

    Check vital signs, including temperature

    • Upper-arm blood pressure: standard measurement and comparison of both arms ↗
    • Temperature measurement Technique card pending
  2. 02

    Inspect spinal alignment and assess the spinous processes and paraspinal muscles for tenderness to palpation or percussion

    • Midline spinal and paraspinal tenderness ↗
  3. 03

    Lumbar range of motion: flexion, extension, and side bending

    • Modified Schober test: 10 cm above and 5 cm below ↗
    • Lumbar extension and lateral flexion range of motion Technique card pending
  4. 04

    Use the straight leg raise to assess for nerve root irritation

    • Straight leg raise, including the Bragard maneuver ↗
  5. 05

    Lower limb strength and sensation, and knee and ankle reflexes

    • Lower limb strength testing (nerve roots L2–S1) ↗
    • Patellar reflex (L2–L4, primarily L4) ↗
    • Lower limb pinprick: key sensory points for L2–S1 ↗
    • Achilles reflex (primarily S1) ↗
    • Light touch sensation: comparison of both sides ↗
  6. 06

    If cauda equina syndrome is suspected, assess perineal sensation and anal sphincter tone

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Digital rectal examination: consent, preparation, and left lateral positioning ↗
    • Saddle sensation: selective light touch assessment of the perineum ↗
    • Anal sphincter tone: resting resistance and voluntary squeeze ↗
  7. 07

    Assess for a pulsatile abdominal mass and costovertebral angle tenderness

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Costovertebral angle tenderness by fist percussion ↗
    • Palpate for a pulsatile abdominal mass as a screen for abdominal aortic aneurysm Technique card pending
    • Assessment for a pulsatile abdominal mass Technique card pending

Common consultation and examination flow

Expand the opening, general history, and pre- and post-examination items

Opening the consultation

  1. 01

    Greet the patient and confirm their name

    What to say to the patient

    Hello. Could you tell me your name?

  2. 02

    Introduce yourself as a medical student and ask for consent to proceed

    What to say to the patient

    My name is [name], and I'm a medical student. Is it okay if I talk with you about your health today?

  3. 03

    Help the patient feel at ease and ask them to tell you right away if they are uncomfortable

    What to say to the patient

    Please let me know right away if you're uncomfortable at any point.

Before taking the history

  1. 01

    Ask an open-ended question about the reason for the visit

    What to say to the patient

    What brings you in today?

  2. 02

    Check for other concerns and agree on the issues to address today

    What to say to the patient

    Is there anything else that's been bothering you?

After taking the history

  1. 01

    Summarize the main symptoms during the interview and confirm your understanding with the patient

    What to say to the patient

    Let me make sure I've understood. You came in because of [main concern], and you've been experiencing [summary]. Is that right?

  2. 02

    Ask about medical history, including diagnoses, hospitalizations, and surgery

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been diagnosed with or treated for any medical conditions? Have you ever had surgery?

  3. 03

    Ask about current prescription medications, over-the-counter medications, and supplements

    What to say to the patient

    Are you taking any medications or supplements?

  4. 04

    Ask about relevant social history, including alcohol, smoking, work, and lifestyle

    What to say to the patient

    Do you drink alcohol or smoke? What do you do for work?

  5. 05

    Ask about relevant family history

    What to say to the patient

    Does anyone in your family have a similar condition?

  6. 06

    Ask about the last menstrual period and the possibility of pregnancy

    Applies to: women of reproductive age

    What to say to the patient

    When did your last period start? Is there any chance you could be pregnant?

Before examination · when indicated

  1. 01

    Explain the area and method of examination and obtain consent

    What to say to the patient

    I'd like to examine you now. Is that okay?

  2. 02

    Clean your hands before the examination and tell the patient you are doing so

    What to say to the patient

    I'm going to clean my hands first.

  3. 03

    Protect privacy by exposing only the area being examined and keeping the rest covered

    What to say to the patient

    I'll only uncover the area I need to examine, and I'll cover you back up right afterward.

After examination · if performed

  1. 01

    Summarize and explain the examination findings to the patient

    What to say to the patient

    When I examined you, there was tenderness when I pressed on [area].
