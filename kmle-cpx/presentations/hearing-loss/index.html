Prioritize the patient's condition. Sudden hearing loss requires prompt specialist assessment even if otoscopy or tuning fork testing is normal. Prioritize urgent evaluation if neurologic symptoms or severe dizziness are also present.

Hearing loss: history questions and examples

  1. 01

    Onset; sudden development within days versus gradual progression

    What to say to the patient

    When did you first notice trouble hearing? Did it happen suddenly or get worse gradually?

  2. 02

    Unilateral or bilateral symptoms; differences between sides

    What to say to the patient

    Is the hearing problem in one ear or both? Which side is worse?

  3. 03

    Situations in which hearing is difficult, such as conversation in noise or high-pitched sounds; television volume

    What to say to the patient

    When is it hardest to hear? Is conversation difficult in noisy places, or do you turn the TV up loud?

  4. 04

    Ear fullness or a blocked sensation

    What to say to the patient

    Does your ear feel full, as if it's blocked?

  5. 05

    Tinnitus: unilateral or pulsatile

    What to say to the patient

    Do you hear a sound in your ear? Is it only on one side, or does it throb in time with your heartbeat?

  6. 06

    Vertigo and its duration; unsteadiness when walking

    What to say to the patient

    Do you get dizzy as if the room is spinning? How long does it last? Do you feel unsteady when you walk?

  7. 07

    Ear pain or discharge

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had ear pain or anything draining from your ear?

  8. 08

    Recent cold or nasal congestion; flying or diving

    What to say to the patient

    Have you recently had a cold or stuffy nose? Have you flown or been diving?

  9. 09

    Cotton swab or ear pick use; onset after showering or swimming

    What to say to the patient

    Do you often clean inside your ears with cotton swabs? Did this start after showering or swimming?

  10. 10

    Facial numbness or weakness, headache, limb weakness, or slurred speech

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had numbness or drooping on one side of your face, weakness in an arm or leg, or slurred speech?

  11. 11

    Occupational or recreational noise exposure: factory work, shooting, or earphones

    What to say to the patient

    Is your workplace noisy, or do you listen to loud music through earphones for long periods?

  12. 12

    Ototoxic medications: aminoglycosides, cisplatin, high-dose aspirin, or loop diuretics

    What to say to the patient

    Have you recently received injected antibiotics or chemotherapy, or taken a lot of aspirin?

  13. 13

    Head or ear trauma

    What to say to the patient

    Have you ever injured your head or ear?

  14. 14

    History of recurrent otitis media or ear surgery

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had frequent middle ear infections since childhood or ever had ear surgery?

  15. 15

    Family history of hearing loss at a young age

    What to say to the patient

    Has anyone in your family had hearing loss from a young age?

  16. 16

    Difficulty with conversation or phone calls; avoiding social gatherings; mood changes

    What to say to the patient

    Has trouble hearing made conversations or phone calls difficult, or led you to avoid seeing people?

  17. 17

    For children: delayed speech, no response when called, school functioning

    Applies to: children

    What to say to the patient

    Is your child slow to respond when called, or is their speech delayed compared with other children? Has their teacher said they have trouble hearing at school?

Which physical examinations should I consider?

These are the app's examination items for this clinical presentation. Use the available technique cards to review how to perform them. Specific examinations without a card remain in the checklist.

  1. 01

    Use an otoscope to check for cerumen, tympanic membrane perforation, and middle ear effusion, including bubbles or an air-fluid level

    • Adult otoscopy: safe approach and tympanic membrane landmarks ↗
  2. 02

    Screen hearing with the whispered voice test

    • Whispered voice test — one ear at a time ↗
  3. 03

    Use Weber and Rinne tuning fork tests to help distinguish conductive from sensorineural hearing loss

    • Weber test — sound lateralization ↗
    • Rinne test — comparing air and bone conduction ↗
  4. 04

    Assess facial sensation, facial nerve function, and cerebellar function

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Finger-to-nose test ↗
    • Facial movements: comparison of the upper and lower face ↗
    • Trigeminal nerve · light facial touch and chewing movements ↗
  5. 05

    If dizziness is present, assess nystagmus and perform the Romberg test

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Romberg test ↗
    • Extraocular movements: six diagnostic positions of gaze ↗

Common consultation and examination flow

Expand the opening, general history, and pre- and post-examination items

Opening the consultation

  1. 01

    Greet the patient and confirm their name

    What to say to the patient

    Hello. Could you tell me your name?

  2. 02

    Introduce yourself as a medical student and ask for consent to proceed

    What to say to the patient

    My name is [name], and I'm a medical student. Is it okay if I talk with you about your health today?

  3. 03

    Help the patient feel at ease and ask them to tell you right away if they are uncomfortable

    What to say to the patient

    Please let me know right away if you're uncomfortable at any point.

Before taking the history

  1. 01

    Ask an open-ended question about the reason for the visit

    What to say to the patient

    What brings you in today?

  2. 02

    Check for other concerns and agree on the issues to address today

    What to say to the patient

    Is there anything else that's been bothering you?

After taking the history

  1. 01

    Summarize the main symptoms during the interview and confirm your understanding with the patient

    What to say to the patient

    Let me make sure I've understood. You came in because of [main concern], and you've been experiencing [summary]. Is that right?

  2. 02

    Ask about medical history, including diagnoses, hospitalizations, and surgery

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been diagnosed with or treated for any medical conditions? Have you ever had surgery?

  3. 03

    Ask about current prescription medications, over-the-counter medications, and supplements

    What to say to the patient

    Are you taking any medications or supplements?

  4. 04

    Ask about relevant social history, including alcohol, smoking, work, and lifestyle

    What to say to the patient

    Do you drink alcohol or smoke? What do you do for work?

  5. 05

    Ask about relevant family history

    What to say to the patient

    Does anyone in your family have a similar condition?

  6. 06

    Ask about the last menstrual period and the possibility of pregnancy

    Applies to: women of reproductive age

    What to say to the patient

    When did your last period start? Is there any chance you could be pregnant?

Before examination · when indicated

  1. 01

    Explain the area and method of examination and obtain consent

    What to say to the patient

    I'd like to examine you now. Is that okay?

  2. 02

    Clean your hands before the examination and tell the patient you are doing so

    What to say to the patient

    I'm going to clean my hands first.

  3. 03

    Protect privacy by exposing only the area being examined and keeping the rest covered

    What to say to the patient

    I'll only uncover the area I need to examine, and I'll cover you back up right afterward.

After examination · if performed

  1. 01

    Summarize and explain the examination findings to the patient

    What to say to the patient

    When I examined you, there was tenderness when I pressed on [area].
