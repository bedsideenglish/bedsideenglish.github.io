Prioritize the patient's condition. With signs of hypertensive emergency suggesting target-organ injury, such as severe headache, visual disturbance, tearing chest or back pain, unilateral weakness, or shortness of breath, prioritize immediate emergency care and assessment over completing the examination checklist.

High blood pressure: history-taking questions and sample wording

  1. 01

    Blood pressure values, measurement location and frequency, and when hypertension was first noted

    What to say to the patient

    What blood pressure reading were you told you had at your checkup? Have you been told it was high before?

  2. 02

    Blood pressure measured outside the clinic, such as at home or at a pharmacy

    What to say to the patient

    Have you checked your blood pressure at home or at a pharmacy? What readings did you get?

  3. 03

    Circumstances during measurement: recent rushing or anxiety, smoking or coffee just beforehand, pain, or lack of sleep

    What to say to the patient

    Were you rushing or feeling very nervous when your blood pressure was measured? Had you just had coffee or smoked?

  4. 04

    Symptoms suggesting hypertensive emergency: severe headache, blurred vision, tearing chest or back pain, shortness of breath, unilateral weakness or slurred speech, and decreased consciousness

    What to say to the patient

    Do you have a severe headache or blurred vision right now? Any tearing pain in your chest or back, shortness of breath, or weakness on one side?

  5. 05

    Symptoms of chronic organ damage: exertional breathlessness or chest tightness, orthopnea, leg edema, foamy urine or nocturia, and calf pain with walking

    What to say to the patient

    Do you get short of breath or feel chest tightness on stairs? Have your legs been swollen, or has your urine been foamy?

  6. 06

    Course suggesting secondary hypertension: onset before age 30, a sudden rise in pressure, or poor control despite multiple medications

    What to say to the patient

    How old were you when you first learned your blood pressure was high? Has it risen suddenly recently, or stayed high despite medication?

  7. 07

    Recurrent episodes of headache, palpitations, sweating, and pallor

    What to say to the patient

    Do you have repeated episodes of sudden headache, a racing heartbeat, and sweating?

  8. 08

    Muscle weakness, leg cramps, and increased urine output as clues to hypokalemia

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had muscle weakness or frequent leg cramps? Are you urinating more often than before?

  9. 09

    Predominantly facial or abdominal weight gain, easy bruising, heat or cold intolerance, and weight changes

    What to say to the patient

    Have you gained weight mainly in your face or abdomen, or been bruising easily? Are you more sensitive to heat or cold than before?

  10. 10

    Loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing during sleep, daytime sleepiness, and morning headache

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been told you snore loudly or stop breathing during sleep? Are you very sleepy during the day?

  11. 11

    History of kidney or urinary disease, hematuria, or proteinuria

    What to say to the patient

    Have you ever been told you have kidney disease, blood in your urine, or protein in your urine?

  12. 12

    Medications or substances that raise blood pressure, including NSAIDs, corticosteroids, oral contraceptives, decongestants, licorice-containing herbal remedies, appetite suppressants, and heavy alcohol use

    What to say to the patient

    Do you often take pain relievers, cold or decongestant medicines, birth control pills, or herbal remedies?

  13. 13

    Cardiovascular risk assessment: diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking, and previous myocardial infarction or stroke

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been told you have diabetes or high cholesterol? Have you ever had heart disease or a stroke?

  14. 14

    Sodium intake, including soup broth, kimchi or salted seafood, instant noodles, processed foods, and frequent restaurant meals

    What to say to the patient

    Do you drink a lot of soup broth or eat salty foods? How often do you have instant noodles or eat out?

  15. 15

    Physical activity and recent weight gain

    What to say to the patient

    How much exercise do you get in a week? Have you gained weight recently?

  16. 16

    Hypertension in parents or siblings; early-onset heart disease, stroke, or kidney disease

    What to say to the patient

    Do your parents or siblings have high blood pressure, or have they had heart disease or a stroke at a young age?

  17. 17

    Future pregnancy plans, as some antihypertensive medications are contraindicated in pregnancy

    Select as clinically indicated

    Applies to: women of reproductive age

    What to say to the patient

    Are you planning a pregnancy? I'm asking because that matters when we choose a medication.

Which physical examinations should I consider?

These are the app's examination items for this clinical presentation. Use the available technique cards to review how to perform them. Specific examinations without a card remain in the checklist.

  1. 01

    After 5 minutes of seated rest, measure blood pressure in both arms with an appropriately sized cuff; take at least two readings 1–2 minutes apart and average them, using the arm with the higher pressure

    • Upper-arm blood pressure: standard measurement and comparison of both arms ↗
  2. 02

    Height, weight, BMI, waist circumference, and pulse

    • Radial pulse: rate and rhythm ↗
    • Anthropometric measurements: height, weight, BMI, and waist circumference Technique card pending
  3. 03

    Perform fundoscopy for retinal vascular changes, hemorrhages or exudates, and optic disc edema

    • Direct ophthalmoscopy: approach and viewing the fundus ↗
  4. 04

    Assess apical impulse location, heart sounds including S4 or murmurs, lung crackles, neck vein distention, and leg edema

    • Precordial palpation: apical impulse, heaves, and thrills ↗
    • Cardiac auscultation areas (aortic, pulmonic, tricuspid, mitral) ↗
    • Posterior lung auscultation sequence ↗
    • Assessment of pitting edema ↗
    • Jugular venous pressure (JVP) assessment ↗
  5. 05

    Auscultate for cervical or abdominal bruits, and compare radial and femoral pulses simultaneously for delay

    • Radiofemoral delay ↗
    • Auscultate for abdominal and carotid bruits Technique card pending
  6. 06

    Palpate the thyroid and look for Cushing features, including a rounded face, wide purple striae, and bruising

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Thyroid palpation from behind while the patient swallows ↗
    • Inspect for Cushing features: a rounded face, purple striae, and bruising Technique card pending

Common consultation and examination flow

Expand the opening, general history, and pre- and post-examination items

Opening the consultation

  1. 01

    Greet the patient and confirm their name

    What to say to the patient

    Hello. Could you tell me your name?

  2. 02

    Introduce yourself as a medical student and ask for consent to proceed

    What to say to the patient

    My name is [name], and I'm a medical student. Is it okay if I talk with you about your health today?

  3. 03

    Help the patient feel at ease and ask them to tell you right away if they are uncomfortable

    What to say to the patient

    Please let me know right away if you're uncomfortable at any point.

Before taking the history

  1. 01

    Ask an open-ended question about the reason for the visit

    What to say to the patient

    What brings you in today?

  2. 02

    Check for other concerns and agree on the issues to address today

    What to say to the patient

    Is there anything else that's been bothering you?

After taking the history

  1. 01

    Summarize the main symptoms during the interview and confirm your understanding with the patient

    What to say to the patient

    Let me make sure I've understood. You came in because of [main concern], and you've been experiencing [summary]. Is that right?

  2. 02

    Ask about medical history, including diagnoses, hospitalizations, and surgery

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been diagnosed with or treated for any medical conditions? Have you ever had surgery?

  3. 03

    Ask about current prescription medications, over-the-counter medications, and supplements

    What to say to the patient

    Are you taking any medications or supplements?

  4. 04

    Ask about relevant social history, including alcohol, smoking, work, and lifestyle

    What to say to the patient

    Do you drink alcohol or smoke? What do you do for work?

  5. 05

    Ask about relevant family history

    What to say to the patient

    Does anyone in your family have a similar condition?

  6. 06

    Ask about the last menstrual period and the possibility of pregnancy

    Applies to: women of reproductive age

    What to say to the patient

    When did your last period start? Is there any chance you could be pregnant?

Before examination · when indicated

  1. 01

    Explain the area and method of examination and obtain consent

    What to say to the patient

    I'd like to examine you now. Is that okay?

  2. 02

    Clean your hands before the examination and tell the patient you are doing so

    What to say to the patient

    I'm going to clean my hands first.

  3. 03

    Protect privacy by exposing only the area being examined and keeping the rest covered

    What to say to the patient

    I'll only uncover the area I need to examine, and I'll cover you back up right afterward.

After examination · if performed

  1. 01

    Summarize and explain the examination findings to the patient

    What to say to the patient

    When I examined you, there was tenderness when I pressed on [area].
