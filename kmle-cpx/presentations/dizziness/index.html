Prioritize the patient's condition. Prioritize urgent assessment for sudden-onset persistent dizziness with new neurologic findings, severe headache, or instability that makes it difficult to stand unaided. Do not delay assessment to finish positional or gait tests, and take precautions to prevent falls.

Dizziness: history-taking questions and sample wording

  1. 01

    Type of dizziness: spinning vertigo, presyncopal lightheadedness, imbalance, or nonspecific dizziness

    What to say to the patient

    What do you mean by dizzy? Does the room spin, or do you feel lightheaded, as though you might pass out?

  2. 02

    Time and pattern of onset, and whether this is the first or a recurrent episode

    What to say to the patient

    When did the dizziness start? Have you had it before?

  3. 03

    Duration of each dizziness episode: seconds to 1 minute, minutes to hours, or continuously for days

    What to say to the patient

    How long does the dizziness last each time? Does it stop within seconds, or continue for hours?

  4. 04

    Triggers: turning the head, lying down, rolling over in bed, or standing up from sitting

    What to say to the patient

    Do you get dizzy when you roll over in bed or look up, or when you stand up?

  5. 05

    Nausea and vomiting

    What to say to the patient

    When you're dizzy, do you feel nauseated or vomit?

  6. 06

    Hearing loss, tinnitus, and ear fullness

    What to say to the patient

    Have you noticed hearing loss, ringing, or a feeling of fullness in your ears?

  7. 07

    Double vision, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, facial or limb numbness or weakness, and inability to walk unaided

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had double vision, slurred speech, or weakness in your arms or legs? Can you walk on your own?

  8. 08

    New severe headache or neck pain

    What to say to the patient

    When you're dizzy, do you also have a severe headache or pain in the back of your neck?

  9. 09

    Palpitations, chest pain, and syncope

    What to say to the patient

    When you're dizzy, do you have a racing heartbeat or chest pain? Have you passed out?

  10. 10

    Recent viral infection, such as a cold

    What to say to the patient

    Did you have a cold before the dizziness started?

  11. 11

    Bleeding, including black stools or heavy periods; reduced food or fluid intake; vomiting or diarrhea

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had trouble eating or drinking enough lately? Have you had any black stools?

  12. 12

    Use of diabetes medications or insulin, and missed meals

    What to say to the patient

    Do you take medication for diabetes? Have you felt dizzy after skipping a meal?

  13. 13

    History of migraine, and sensitivity to light or sound during dizziness

    What to say to the patient

    Do you get migraines? When you're dizzy, does light or sound bother you?

  14. 14

    Stroke risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and atrial fibrillation

    What to say to the patient

    Do you have high blood pressure or diabetes? Have you ever been told you have an irregular heartbeat?

  15. 15

    Recent addition or change of medications, including antihypertensives, diuretics, sedatives or sleeping pills, and antiseizure medications

    What to say to the patient

    Have you started or changed any medications recently? What about blood pressure medication or sleeping pills?

  16. 16

    Anxiety, hyperventilation, and stress

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been under a lot of stress, or do you get dizzy when you're anxious?

Which physical examinations should I consider?

These are the app's examination items for this clinical presentation. Use the available technique cards to review how to perform them. Specific examinations without a card remain in the checklist.

  1. 01

    Measure blood pressure and pulse while supine and standing

    • Orthostatic blood pressure and pulse measurement ↗
  2. 02

    Observe nystagmus, including its direction and changes with gaze direction

    • Extraocular movements: six diagnostic positions of gaze ↗
  3. 03

    In acute persistent vertigo with nystagmus, a clinician trained in HINTS uses head impulse, nystagmus assessment, and test of skew to help distinguish peripheral from central causes

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Head impulse test (HINTS) ↗
    • Alternate cover test: vertical refixation (skew) ↗
  4. 04

    Use the Dix-Hallpike maneuver to assess for vertigo and nystagmus triggered by a change in head position

    • Dix-Hallpike maneuver ↗
  5. 05

    Cranial nerve examination, finger-to-nose testing, Romberg testing, and tandem gait

    • Finger-to-nose test ↗
    • Romberg test ↗
    • Gait and tandem gait ↗
    • Detailed cranial nerve motor and sensory examination Technique card pending
  6. 06

    Assess pulse regularity and auscultate the heart for murmurs

    • Radial pulse: rate and rhythm ↗
    • Cardiac auscultation areas (aortic, pulmonic, tricuspid, mitral) ↗

Common consultation and examination flow

Expand the opening, general history, and pre- and post-examination items

Opening the consultation

  1. 01

    Greet the patient and confirm their name

    What to say to the patient

    Hello. Could you tell me your name?

  2. 02

    Introduce yourself as a medical student and ask for consent to proceed

    What to say to the patient

    My name is [name], and I'm a medical student. Is it okay if I talk with you about your health today?

  3. 03

    Help the patient feel at ease and ask them to tell you right away if they are uncomfortable

    What to say to the patient

    Please let me know right away if you're uncomfortable at any point.

Before taking the history

  1. 01

    Ask an open-ended question about the reason for the visit

    What to say to the patient

    What brings you in today?

  2. 02

    Check for other concerns and agree on the issues to address today

    What to say to the patient

    Is there anything else that's been bothering you?

After taking the history

  1. 01

    Summarize the main symptoms during the interview and confirm your understanding with the patient

    What to say to the patient

    Let me make sure I've understood. You came in because of [main concern], and you've been experiencing [summary]. Is that right?

  2. 02

    Ask about medical history, including diagnoses, hospitalizations, and surgery

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been diagnosed with or treated for any medical conditions? Have you ever had surgery?

  3. 03

    Ask about current prescription medications, over-the-counter medications, and supplements

    What to say to the patient

    Are you taking any medications or supplements?

  4. 04

    Ask about relevant social history, including alcohol, smoking, work, and lifestyle

    What to say to the patient

    Do you drink alcohol or smoke? What do you do for work?

  5. 05

    Ask about relevant family history

    What to say to the patient

    Does anyone in your family have a similar condition?

  6. 06

    Ask about the last menstrual period and the possibility of pregnancy

    Applies to: women of reproductive age

    What to say to the patient

    When did your last period start? Is there any chance you could be pregnant?

Before examination · when indicated

  1. 01

    Explain the area and method of examination and obtain consent

    What to say to the patient

    I'd like to examine you now. Is that okay?

  2. 02

    Clean your hands before the examination and tell the patient you are doing so

    What to say to the patient

    I'm going to clean my hands first.

  3. 03

    Protect privacy by exposing only the area being examined and keeping the rest covered

    What to say to the patient

    I'll only uncover the area I need to examine, and I'll cover you back up right afterward.

After examination · if performed

  1. 01

    Summarize and explain the examination findings to the patient

    What to say to the patient

    When I examined you, there was tenderness when I pressed on [area].

About this material and its references

The history questions and examination checklists are organized from the Bedside English app's study material for each clinical presentation. Review the common consultation flow and see Dizziness examination referencesfor examination techniques and references.

Clinical context

HINTS requires an appropriately trained examiner and the right clinical setting. It is not a general screening test for every type of dizziness. NICE: sudden-onset acute vestibular syndrome.
