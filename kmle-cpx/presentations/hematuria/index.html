Prioritize the patient's condition. Prioritize urgent assessment for hematuria with inability to urinate, severe pain or fever, syncope, or circulatory instability. Persistent or recurrent painless visible hematuria requires further assessment even when the physical examination is normal.

Red urine and hematuria: history-taking questions and sample wording

  1. 01

    Onset, persistent or recurrent symptoms, and previous similar episodes

    What to say to the patient

    When did your urine first look red? Has this happened before?

  2. 02

    Urine color (bright red or cola-colored) and presence of clots

    What to say to the patient

    Is your urine bright red, or dark brown like cola? Have you passed any blood clots?

  3. 03

    Timing of visible blood within the urinary stream: initial, terminal, or throughout

    What to say to the patient

    Is the urine red only at the beginning, only at the end, or throughout the stream?

  4. 04

    Presence or absence of pain, including painless hematuria

    What to say to the patient

    When your urine is red, do you have pain or discomfort, or is it completely painless?

  5. 05

    Flank pain and radiation to the groin

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had pain in your side? Does it spread to your lower abdomen or groin?

  6. 06

    Dysuria, frequency, and urgency

    What to say to the patient

    Does it hurt when you urinate, or do you need to go often?

  7. 07

    Fever and chills

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had a fever or felt chilled?

  8. 08

    Difficulty starting urination, weak stream, and a sense of incomplete emptying

    What to say to the patient

    Has your urine stream become weaker, or does your bladder still feel full after you go?

  9. 09

    Facial or leg edema, foamy urine, and decreased urine output

    What to say to the patient

    Have your face or legs been swollen, or has your urine been foamy or decreased in amount?

  10. 10

    Interval between a recent throat or skin infection and hematuria

    What to say to the patient

    Have you recently had a sore throat or skin infection? How many days later did your urine turn red?

  11. 11

    Systemic symptoms, including rash, joint pain, cough, or hemoptysis

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had a rash, joint pain, or coughed up blood?

  12. 12

    Weight loss and fatigue

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been losing weight or feeling very tired recently?

  13. 13

    Nosebleeds, bleeding gums, and easy bruising

    What to say to the patient

    Do you get nosebleeds or bleeding gums, or bruise easily?

  14. 14

    Anticoagulant or antiplatelet use

    What to say to the patient

    Do you take a blood thinner or aspirin?

  15. 15

    Strenuous exercise, trauma, and intake of red-colored foods or medications

    What to say to the patient

    Have you exercised very intensely or had an injury recently? Have you eaten red foods, such as beets, or started any new medications?

  16. 16

    Current menstruation, to assess for contamination with menstrual blood

    Applies to: women of reproductive age

    What to say to the patient

    Are you on your period now, or did it finish recently?

  17. 17

    Personal and family history of urinary stones or kidney disease

    What to say to the patient

    Have you ever had kidney stones or kidney disease? Is anyone in your family on dialysis?

  18. 18

    Smoking history and occupational exposure to dyes, rubber, or chemicals

    What to say to the patient

    How many years have you smoked, and how much do you smoke a day? Do you work with dyes or chemicals?

Which physical examinations should I consider?

These are the app's examination items for this clinical presentation. Use the available technique cards to review how to perform them. Specific examinations without a card remain in the checklist.

  1. 01

    Check blood pressure and temperature for hypertension associated with glomerulonephritis or fever associated with infection

    • Radial pulse: rate and rhythm ↗
    • Upper-arm blood pressure: standard measurement and comparison of both arms ↗
    • Temperature measurement Technique card pending
  2. 02

    Check for periorbital and pretibial edema

    • General inspection from the end of the bed ↗
    • Assessment of pitting edema ↗
  3. 03

    Palpate the abdomen for masses, bladder distention, and tenderness

    • Light and deep abdominal palpation ↗
  4. 04

    Check for costovertebral angle tenderness bilaterally

    • Costovertebral angle tenderness by fist percussion ↗
  5. 05

    Check the skin for bruises, petechiae, and rash

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Rash blanching assessment: petechiae and purpura ↗
    • Assess the morphology and distribution of any associated rash Technique card pending
  6. 06

    In male patients, perform a digital rectal examination if indicated to assess prostate size and nodules

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Digital rectal examination: consent, preparation, and left lateral positioning ↗
    • Digital rectal examination: rectal wall and prostate ↗

Common consultation and examination flow

Expand the opening, general history, and pre- and post-examination items

Opening the consultation

  1. 01

    Greet the patient and confirm their name

    What to say to the patient

    Hello. Could you tell me your name?

  2. 02

    Introduce yourself as a medical student and ask for consent to proceed

    What to say to the patient

    My name is [name], and I'm a medical student. Is it okay if I talk with you about your health today?

  3. 03

    Help the patient feel at ease and ask them to tell you right away if they are uncomfortable

    What to say to the patient

    Please let me know right away if you're uncomfortable at any point.

Before taking the history

  1. 01

    Ask an open-ended question about the reason for the visit

    What to say to the patient

    What brings you in today?

  2. 02

    Check for other concerns and agree on the issues to address today

    What to say to the patient

    Is there anything else that's been bothering you?

After taking the history

  1. 01

    Summarize the main symptoms during the interview and confirm your understanding with the patient

    What to say to the patient

    Let me make sure I've understood. You came in because of [main concern], and you've been experiencing [summary]. Is that right?

  2. 02

    Ask about medical history, including diagnoses, hospitalizations, and surgery

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been diagnosed with or treated for any medical conditions? Have you ever had surgery?

  3. 03

    Ask about current prescription medications, over-the-counter medications, and supplements

    What to say to the patient

    Are you taking any medications or supplements?

  4. 04

    Ask about relevant social history, including alcohol, smoking, work, and lifestyle

    What to say to the patient

    Do you drink alcohol or smoke? What do you do for work?

  5. 05

    Ask about relevant family history

    What to say to the patient

    Does anyone in your family have a similar condition?

  6. 06

    Ask about the last menstrual period and the possibility of pregnancy

    Applies to: women of reproductive age

    What to say to the patient

    When did your last period start? Is there any chance you could be pregnant?

Before examination · when indicated

  1. 01

    Explain the area and method of examination and obtain consent

    What to say to the patient

    I'd like to examine you now. Is that okay?

  2. 02

    Clean your hands before the examination and tell the patient you are doing so

    What to say to the patient

    I'm going to clean my hands first.

  3. 03

    Protect privacy by exposing only the area being examined and keeping the rest covered

    What to say to the patient

    I'll only uncover the area I need to examine, and I'll cover you back up right afterward.

After examination · if performed

  1. 01

    Summarize and explain the examination findings to the patient

    What to say to the patient

    When I examined you, there was tenderness when I pressed on [area].
