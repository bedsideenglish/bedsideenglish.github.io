Prioritize the patient's condition. With hemodynamic instability, sudden severe pain, or clear signs of peritoneal irritation, avoid repeated provocative maneuvers and arrange immediate emergency assessment. A normal examination does not rule out life-threatening conditions such as acute appendicitis or mesenteric ischemia.

Acute abdominal pain: history-taking questions and sample wording

  1. 01

    Time and pattern of onset (sudden or gradual)

    What to say to the patient

    When did the pain start? Did it start suddenly?

  2. 02

    Pain location and migration from the initial site

    What to say to the patient

    Where does it hurt the most? Is that where it hurt when it first started?

  3. 03

    Radiation to the back, shoulder, or groin

    What to say to the patient

    Does the pain spread to your back, shoulder, or groin?

  4. 04

    Pain pattern: constant or intermittent and cramping

    What to say to the patient

    What does the pain feel like? Is it constant, or does it come and go in cramps?

  5. 05

    Pain severity and change over time

    What to say to the patient

    If the worst pain was a 10, what number would you give it now? Is it getting worse?

  6. 06

    Aggravating and relieving factors: meals, movement, and position

    What to say to the patient

    Does eating or moving make it worse? Is there a position that makes it feel better?

  7. 07

    Previous similar pain

    What to say to the patient

    Have you ever had pain like this before?

  8. 08

    Nausea, vomiting, and vomit characteristics, including bile or blood

    What to say to the patient

    Have you felt nauseated or vomited? What color was the vomit?

  9. 09

    Ability to pass stool and gas, diarrhea, and blood in the stool

    What to say to the patient

    Are you able to have bowel movements and pass gas? Have you had diarrhea or blood in your stool?

  10. 10

    Fever and chills

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had a fever or shaking chills?

  11. 11

    Jaundice, dark urine, and pale stools

    What to say to the patient

    Have your eyes or skin looked yellow, or has your urine been darker than usual?

  12. 12

    Dysuria, urinary frequency, and hematuria

    What to say to the patient

    Does it hurt when you urinate, or have you noticed blood in your urine?

  13. 13

    Dizziness or syncope, raising concern for intra-abdominal bleeding

    What to say to the patient

    Have you felt dizzy or as though you might pass out?

  14. 14

    Vaginal bleeding and discharge

    Applies to: female patients

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had any vaginal bleeding or an increase in discharge?

  15. 15

    Chest pain and shortness of breath, considering myocardial infarction or lower-lobe pneumonia

    What to say to the patient

    Have you had any chest pain or shortness of breath?

  16. 16

    Food eaten before the pain, including fatty foods, and alcohol use

    What to say to the patient

    What did you eat before the pain started? Had you had any alcohol?

  17. 17

    Use of NSAIDs or aspirin

    What to say to the patient

    Do you take pain relievers or aspirin often?

  18. 18

    Arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation, and vascular disease (risk of embolism or ischemia)

    Select as clinically indicated

    What to say to the patient

    Have you ever been told you have an irregular heartbeat or a blood vessel condition?

Which physical examinations should I consider?

These are the app's examination items for this clinical presentation. Use the available technique cards to review how to perform them. Specific examinations without a card remain in the checklist.

  1. 01

    Check blood pressure, pulse, and temperature for signs of shock or fever

    • Upper-arm blood pressure: standard measurement and comparison of both arms ↗
    • Temperature measurement Technique card pending
    • Possibility of pregnancy and pregnancy testing Technique card pending
  2. 02

    Inspect the abdomen for distention and surgical scars, and auscultate bowel sounds

    • Abdominal inspection ↗
    • Abdominal auscultation: bowel sounds ↗
  3. 03

    Starting away from the pain, palpate lightly and then deeply to locate tenderness

    • Abdominal percussion: tympany and dullness ↗
    • Light and deep abdominal palpation ↗
  4. 04

    Assess for signs of peritoneal irritation, including rebound tenderness and rigidity

    • Rebound tenderness (Blumberg sign) ↗
    • Peritoneal irritation: coughing and gentle percussion ↗
  5. 05

    Perform targeted maneuvers, such as Murphy sign or McBurney point tenderness assessment, based on the suspected site

    • McBurney point tenderness ↗
    • Murphy sign ↗
    • Rovsing sign ↗
    • Psoas sign ↗
  6. 06

    Check for costovertebral angle tenderness on both sides

    • Costovertebral angle tenderness by fist percussion ↗
  7. 07

    Explain that a pelvic or digital rectal examination may be needed if clinically indicated

    Select as clinically indicated

    • Digital rectal examination: consent, preparation, and left lateral positioning ↗
    • Digital rectal examination: rectal wall and prostate ↗
    • Speculum examination: vaginal approach and cervical view ↗
    • Bimanual pelvic examination: cervix, uterus, and adnexa ↗

Common consultation and examination flow

Expand the opening, general history, and pre- and post-examination items

Opening the consultation

  1. 01

    Greet the patient and confirm their name

    What to say to the patient

    Hello. Could you tell me your name?

  2. 02

    Introduce yourself as a medical student and ask for consent to proceed

    What to say to the patient

    My name is [name], and I'm a medical student. Is it okay if I talk with you about your health today?

  3. 03

    Help the patient feel at ease and ask them to tell you right away if they are uncomfortable

    What to say to the patient

    Please let me know right away if you're uncomfortable at any point.

Before taking the history

  1. 01

    Ask an open-ended question about the reason for the visit

    What to say to the patient

    What brings you in today?

  2. 02

    Check for other concerns and agree on the issues to address today

    What to say to the patient

    Is there anything else that's been bothering you?

After taking the history

  1. 01

    Summarize the main symptoms during the interview and confirm your understanding with the patient

    What to say to the patient

    Let me make sure I've understood. You came in because of [main concern], and you've been experiencing [summary]. Is that right?

  2. 02

    Ask about medical history, including diagnoses, hospitalizations, and surgery

    What to say to the patient

    Have you been diagnosed with or treated for any medical conditions? Have you ever had surgery?

  3. 03

    Ask about current prescription medications, over-the-counter medications, and supplements

    What to say to the patient

    Are you taking any medications or supplements?

  4. 04

    Ask about relevant social history, including alcohol, smoking, work, and lifestyle

    What to say to the patient

    Do you drink alcohol or smoke? What do you do for work?

  5. 05

    Ask about relevant family history

    What to say to the patient

    Does anyone in your family have a similar condition?

  6. 06

    Ask about the last menstrual period and the possibility of pregnancy

    Applies to: women of reproductive age

    What to say to the patient

    When did your last period start? Is there any chance you could be pregnant?

Before examination · when indicated

  1. 01

    Explain the area and method of examination and obtain consent

    What to say to the patient

    I'd like to examine you now. Is that okay?

  2. 02

    Clean your hands before the examination and tell the patient you are doing so

    What to say to the patient

    I'm going to clean my hands first.

  3. 03

    Protect privacy by exposing only the area being examined and keeping the rest covered

    What to say to the patient

    I'll only uncover the area I need to examine, and I'll cover you back up right afterward.

After examination · if performed

  1. 01

    Summarize and explain the examination findings to the patient

    What to say to the patient

    When I examined you, there was tenderness when I pressed on [area].
