Quick answer

How should I present an acute pancreatitis case on rounds?

Lead with the boring epigastric pain radiating to the back after alcohol, since the location, radiation, and trigger together suggest the diagnosis. Give the markedly elevated lipase, BUN, and near-normal ALT before naming acute pancreatitis. End with fluid resuscitation, analgesia, early oral feeding as tolerated, and the severity and cause assessment still needed.

Before you speak

Define the moment and the information you actually have

Setting

An urgent presentation once the stat pancreatitis labs are back, before severity scoring or imaging.

Audience

A supervising physician deciding on fluid resuscitation and cause assessment.

Available data

History, social history, serum lipase, BUN, and ALT. Vital signs, a full severity score, and imaging are not yet supplied.

Target length

About 75 seconds

About 75 seconds practice script

Ms. Campbell is a 38-year-old woman with no significant past medical history who presents with severe, boring epigastric pain radiating to her back and vomiting after alcohol intake.

Her symptoms started recently and have gradually worsened, with associated fatigue, and she suspects this could be pancreatitis herself.

She has no significant past medical history or regular medications, and describes herself as a social drinker, though the amount consumed before this episode is relevant to the working cause.

Her serum lipase is markedly elevated at 1260, her blood urea nitrogen is elevated at 24, and her ALT is 48, essentially within normal range, arguing against a gallstone-related biliary cause.

My diagnosis is acute pancreatitis, most likely alcohol-related given the trigger and the near-normal ALT arguing against gallstones, though the cause still needs to be confirmed with further history and imaging.

I would provide analgesia and start goal-directed intravenous fluid resuscitation, monitoring closely for fluid overload, and begin oral feeding as tolerated rather than keeping her NPO for a prolonged period. I would assess severity and investigate the cause further, including confirming her alcohol intake and considering imaging if gallstones remain a concern.

Build the presentation

Give every sentence one job

A presentation is a compressed clinical argument, not a chart read aloud. These six moves keep the facts connected to your reasoning.

01

Opening one-liner

Name the pain pattern and its likely trigger

“Ms. Campbell is a 38-year-old woman with no significant past medical history who presents with severe, boring epigastric pain radiating to her back and vomiting after alcohol intake.”

Why it belongs: Naming the specific pain quality, its radiation to the back, and the alcohol trigger together in the opening sentence points directly toward pancreatitis before any lab is mentioned.

Less clear

This is a 38-year-old with bad stomach pain after drinking.

Prefer

Ms. Campbell has severe, boring epigastric pain radiating to her back and vomiting after alcohol intake.

Why: Naming the specific pain quality and radiation carries more diagnostic weight than a general description.

02

History of present illness

Confirm the alcohol trigger and her own suspicion

“Her symptoms started recently and have gradually worsened, with associated fatigue, and she suspects this could be pancreatitis herself.”

Why it belongs: Noting that the patient herself suspects pancreatitis is worth stating honestly, while still requiring objective confirmation rather than accepting her impression alone.

Less clear

She thinks it's her pancreas.

Prefer

She suspects this could be pancreatitis herself, though this needs objective confirmation.

Why: Naming the patient's own idea while keeping the clinical conclusion separate is more precise.

03

Relevant background

Confirm there is no other major predisposing condition

“She has no significant past medical history or regular medications, and describes herself as a social drinker, though the amount consumed before this episode is relevant to the working cause.”

Why it belongs: An unremarkable background with alcohol as the likely trigger is different from having known gallstone disease, and this distinction affects the cause-related workup.

Less clear

She drinks socially sometimes.

Prefer

She describes herself as a social drinker, though the exact amount before this episode is relevant to the working cause.

Why: Flagging that the exact amount matters is more useful than a vague social label.

04

Objective data

State the labs that support the diagnosis

“Her serum lipase is markedly elevated at 1260, her blood urea nitrogen is elevated at 24, and her ALT is 48, essentially within normal range, arguing against a gallstone-related biliary cause.”

Why it belongs: A lipase this markedly elevated strongly supports pancreatitis, the elevated BUN is an early marker relevant to severity and fluid status, and a near-normal ALT makes a gallstone cause somewhat less likely, pointing back toward alcohol.

Less clear

Her pancreas enzyme was very high.

Prefer

Her lipase is 1,260, markedly elevated, with a near-normal ALT of 48 arguing against a gallstone cause.

Why: Exact values and naming what the ALT result argues against are more useful than a summary statement.

05

Assessment

Commit to the diagnosis and its likely cause

“My diagnosis is acute pancreatitis, most likely alcohol-related given the trigger and the near-normal ALT arguing against gallstones, though the cause still needs to be confirmed with further history and imaging.”

Why it belongs: The assessment names the most likely cause while explicitly stating that confirmation is still needed, avoiding premature certainty about etiology.

Less clear

This is pancreatitis from alcohol.

Prefer

This is acute pancreatitis, most likely alcohol-related, though the cause still needs confirmation with further history and imaging.

Why: Keeping the cause as 'most likely,' not certain, is more accurate at this stage.

06

Plan

State fluid resuscitation, analgesia, and early feeding

“I would provide analgesia and start goal-directed intravenous fluid resuscitation, monitoring closely for fluid overload, and begin oral feeding as tolerated rather than keeping her NPO for a prolonged period. I would assess severity and investigate the cause further, including confirming her alcohol intake and considering imaging if gallstones remain a concern.”

Why it belongs: Early oral feeding as tolerated, rather than prolonged fasting, reflects current evidence-based management, and naming the ongoing cause investigation shows the assessment is not yet closed.

Less clear

I'll keep her NPO and give fluids.

Prefer

I would start goal-directed IV fluids and analgesia, and begin oral feeding as tolerated rather than prolonged fasting.

Why: Naming early feeding as tolerated corrects the older default of routine prolonged NPO status.

Do not fill the blanks

Say what has not yet been established

Real presentations often happen before the chart is complete. A brief, explicit limitation is safer and more credible than a polished invention.

Vital signs and a formal severity score

Say: “Vital signs and a formal severity score are not documented in this simulation, and I would obtain them to guide the intensity of monitoring and fluid resuscitation.”

Severity assessment should not be assumed from lipase level alone, since lipase does not correlate well with severity.

Imaging and triglyceride level

Say: “Abdominal imaging and a triglyceride level are not part of this simulation and would help confirm or exclude gallstones and hypertriglyceridemia as causes.”

Alcohol is the leading suspicion here, but other treatable causes should still be actively excluded.

After the presentation

Likely attending questions

Answer the question first, then give the evidence. If the source case does not contain the answer, say what you would verify.

Why does the ALT matter here?

A significantly elevated ALT, particularly above about three times normal, raises suspicion for a gallstone-related biliary cause of pancreatitis; her near-normal ALT of 48 makes that less likely and points more toward an alcohol-related cause given her history.

Does the lipase level tell you how severe this is?

Not reliably. Lipase confirms the diagnosis but does not correlate well with severity, so a formal severity score using vital signs, organ function, and other markers is needed rather than inferring severity from the lipase value alone.

Why start oral feeding early instead of keeping her NPO?

Current evidence supports early oral feeding as tolerated in pancreatitis, since prolonged fasting does not improve outcomes and may be harmful; feeding is advanced based on her symptoms and tolerance rather than a fixed NPO period.

Self-check

A 30-second check before you present

  1. Name the pain quality, radiation, and trigger together in the opening sentence.
  2. State the exact lipase value, not just 'elevated.'
  3. Name what the ALT result argues for or against.
  4. State the most likely cause while keeping it labeled as not yet confirmed.
  5. State that severity is assessed with a formal score, not lipase alone.
  6. State that oral feeding is advanced as tolerated, not routinely withheld.
  7. State what imaging or lab tests are still needed to confirm the cause.
  8. Avoid assuming severity from symptoms alone.

Common presentation problems

Repair the habits that make a case hard to follow

Assuming lipase level predicts severity

Repair: State that a formal severity score is used instead of the lipase value alone.

Keeping the patient NPO for a prolonged period by default

Repair: State that oral feeding is being advanced as tolerated.

Calling the cause confirmed as alcohol without further workup

Repair: State that alcohol is the leading suspicion but the cause still needs confirmation.

Omitting the ALT result when discussing the cause

Repair: State the ALT value and what it suggests about a gallstone-related cause.

Frequently asked questions

Oral case presentation questions

Do I need to calculate a specific severity score by name?

Mentioning that a formal severity assessment is planned is usually sufficient; be ready to name the specific score your institution uses if asked.

Should I mention imaging even if it hasn't been done yet?

Yes, stating that imaging will help confirm or exclude gallstones shows you are actively pursuing the cause rather than assuming it.

How long should this presentation be?

This worked example targets about 75 seconds. A patient with signs of severe or complicated pancreatitis would need a longer presentation covering organ-function findings.
