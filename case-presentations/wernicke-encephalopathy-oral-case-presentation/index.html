Wernicke encephalopathy oral case presentation

A source-locked example showing how to present confusion, ataxia, and eye findings in a malnourished patient with alcohol use disorder while treating thiamine as urgent, not optional.

Quick answer

How should I present a suspected Wernicke encephalopathy case on rounds?

Lead with the classic triad—confusion, ataxia, and eye findings—in a patient with severe alcohol use disorder and poor nutrition, since this pattern should trigger immediate treatment. State that glucose and CT are normal before naming Wernicke encephalopathy. End with giving thiamine immediately, explaining that it should not wait for a confirmed low level, and that glucose should still be treated without delay if it were low.

Before you speak

Define the moment and the information you actually have

Setting

An emergency-department presentation immediately after point-of-care glucose and CT head results return, before any lab-confirmed thiamine level.

Audience

A supervising physician deciding on immediate thiamine administration.

Available data

History from neighbors, vital signs, a focused neurologic exam, point-of-care glucose, and CT head. A formal thiamine level and full nutritional panel are not itemized.

Target length

About 80 seconds

Complete spoken example

First, read it once without stopping

The paragraph breaks show the clinical shape. In a real presentation, pause briefly between ideas rather than announcing the headings.

About 80 seconds practice script

Mr. Foster is a 50-year-old man with severe alcohol use disorder who was found confused and unable to walk steadily, now showing confusion, ataxia, and abnormal eye movements.

He has consumed about a liter of vodka daily with minimal food intake for the past three weeks and was found stumbling and confused by neighbors. He is too disoriented to give a clear history and confabulates, incorrectly describing having just returned from a work trip.

His history includes severe alcohol use disorder, alcoholic liver disease, and peripheral neuropathy, and he lives alone with no other medications.

His vitals show a heart rate of 104, blood pressure 110 over 68, and a temperature of 36.6 — normal, in the 36-to-37 range. He has bilateral abnormal eye movements with gaze paresis, severe wide-based ataxia requiring two-person support to stand, and is disoriented with confabulation. Point-of-care glucose is 78, and CT head shows no hemorrhage or acute infarction.

My diagnosis is acute Wernicke encephalopathy, based on the classic triad of encephalopathy, ataxia, and oculomotor findings in a malnourished patient with severe alcohol use disorder, and this needs immediate thiamine treatment rather than waiting for a confirmed low level.

I am giving parenteral thiamine now under our hospital protocol, before or together with any glucose administration, while monitoring and correcting magnesium and other nutritional deficiencies. If his glucose had been low, I would still treat clinically significant hypoglycemia immediately rather than delaying it to give thiamine first. I would also obtain collateral history, since he cannot reliably provide his own, and monitor closely for alcohol withdrawal.

Build the presentation

Give every sentence one job

A presentation is a compressed clinical argument, not a chart read aloud. These six moves keep the facts connected to your reasoning.

01

Opening one-liner

Name the classic triad and the nutritional risk together

“Mr. Foster is a 50-year-old man with severe alcohol use disorder who was found confused and unable to walk steadily, now showing confusion, ataxia, and abnormal eye movements.”

Why it belongs: Naming the classic triad of confusion, ataxia, and eye findings alongside his alcohol use disorder in the opening sentence should immediately raise Wernicke encephalopathy and prompt urgent thiamine, not a routine intoxication work-up.

Less clear

This is a man with alcohol problems who is confused and unsteady.

Prefer

He has confusion, ataxia, and abnormal eye movements in the setting of severe alcohol use disorder and poor nutrition.

Why: Naming the specific triad, not just 'confused and unsteady,' is what should trigger immediate consideration of this diagnosis.

02

History of present illness

Give the nutritional history and note the unreliable narrative

“He has consumed about a liter of vodka daily with minimal food intake for the past three weeks and was found stumbling and confused by neighbors. He is too disoriented to give a clear history and confabulates, incorrectly describing having just returned from a work trip.”

Why it belongs: Naming the specific duration of heavy drinking with minimal food intake establishes the nutritional deficiency risk, and noting his confabulation is itself a clinically meaningful finding, not just an aside.

Less clear

He's been drinking a lot and not eating well.

Prefer

He has consumed about a liter of vodka daily with minimal food intake for three weeks, and he confabulates when questioned.

Why: Naming the specific amount, duration, and the confabulation itself carries diagnostic weight.

03

Relevant background

Name the chronic conditions relevant to his nutritional state

“His history includes severe alcohol use disorder, alcoholic liver disease, and peripheral neuropathy, and he lives alone with no other medications.”

Why it belongs: Alcoholic liver disease and peripheral neuropathy both reflect a chronic pattern of malnutrition and alcohol-related organ damage that supports his risk for thiamine deficiency.

Less clear

He has liver problems from drinking.

Prefer

He has alcoholic liver disease and peripheral neuropathy, both reflecting chronic malnutrition from his alcohol use.

Why: Connecting the chronic conditions to the nutritional risk is more useful than listing them without context.

04

Objective data

State vitals, the neuro exam, and initial results

“His vitals show a heart rate of 104, blood pressure 110 over 68, and a temperature of 36.6 — normal, in the 36-to-37 range. He has bilateral abnormal eye movements with gaze paresis, severe wide-based ataxia requiring two-person support to stand, and is disoriented with confabulation. Point-of-care glucose is 78, and CT head shows no hemorrhage or acute infarction.”

Why it belongs: A normal glucose and CT help exclude hypoglycemia and an acute structural lesion as the sole explanation, which is exactly why the classic triad in this nutritional context points toward Wernicke encephalopathy rather than those more commonly considered causes.

Less clear

His scans and blood sugar were normal.

Prefer

His glucose is 78 and CT head shows no hemorrhage or infarction, which helps exclude hypoglycemia and a structural lesion.

Why: Naming what these normal results specifically exclude is more useful than a general reassurance.

05

Assessment

Commit to the diagnosis and its urgency

“My diagnosis is acute Wernicke encephalopathy, based on the classic triad of encephalopathy, ataxia, and oculomotor findings in a malnourished patient with severe alcohol use disorder, and this needs immediate thiamine treatment rather than waiting for a confirmed low level.”

Why it belongs: Stating that treatment should not wait for a confirmed lab value is the single most important safety point in this presentation, since this is a clinical diagnosis treated empirically.

Less clear

This looks like a vitamin deficiency from his drinking.

Prefer

This is acute Wernicke encephalopathy, and it needs immediate thiamine treatment rather than waiting for a confirmed low level.

Why: Naming the urgency of empiric treatment reflects the correct clinical approach to this diagnosis.

06

Plan

State thiamine timing and glucose safety together

“I am giving parenteral thiamine now under our hospital protocol, before or together with any glucose administration, while monitoring and correcting magnesium and other nutritional deficiencies. If his glucose had been low, I would still treat clinically significant hypoglycemia immediately rather than delaying it to give thiamine first. I would also obtain collateral history, since he cannot reliably provide his own, and monitor closely for alcohol withdrawal.”

Why it belongs: Explicitly stating that significant hypoglycemia is still treated immediately, while thiamine is given before or with glucose whenever feasible, directly addresses a genuine sequencing question that trainees often get backwards in either direction.

Less clear

I'll give him some vitamins and glucose.

Prefer

I am giving thiamine now, before or with any glucose, though clinically significant hypoglycemia would still be treated immediately if present.

Why: Naming the correct sequencing addresses the exact safety question this diagnosis raises.

Do not fill the blanks

Say what has not yet been established

Real presentations often happen before the chart is complete. A brief, explicit limitation is safer and more credible than a polished invention.

Confirmed thiamine level

Say: “A confirmed thiamine level is not available in this simulation, and treatment is not delayed to obtain one, since this remains a clinical diagnosis treated empirically.”

Waiting for a lab-confirmed deficiency before treating risks irreversible neurologic injury.

Alcohol withdrawal severity assessment

Say: “A formal withdrawal severity assessment is not documented in this simulation, and I would begin monitoring for alcohol withdrawal given his heavy recent use.”

He is at risk for withdrawal that would need its own monitoring and treatment protocol alongside the Wernicke treatment.

After the presentation

Likely attending questions

Answer the question first, then give the evidence. If the source case does not contain the answer, say what you would verify.

Why give thiamine before confirming a low level?

Wernicke encephalopathy is a clinical diagnosis, and untreated or delayed treatment risks irreversible brain injury, so thiamine is given empirically based on clinical suspicion rather than waiting for a lab-confirmed deficiency, which would take too long to be safe.

Isn't giving glucose before thiamine dangerous in alcoholic patients?

Glucose administration in a thiamine-deficient patient can precipitate or worsen Wernicke encephalopathy, so thiamine is given before or together with glucose whenever feasible; however, clinically significant hypoglycemia is still treated immediately, since untreated severe hypoglycemia is also dangerous.

Why does his confabulation matter clinically?

Confabulation reflects the encephalopathy component of the syndrome and also means his own history cannot be relied upon alone, which is why obtaining collateral history from neighbors or family is an important part of the plan.

Self-check

A 30-second check before you present

  1. Name the classic triad—confusion, ataxia, eye findings—in the opening sentence.
  2. Quantify the alcohol intake and duration of poor nutrition.
  3. Note confabulation as a clinical finding, not just an aside.
  4. State glucose and CT results and what they specifically exclude.
  5. State that thiamine treatment is not delayed for a confirmed lab level.
  6. State the correct sequencing: thiamine before or with glucose, but hypoglycemia still treated immediately.
  7. Mention collateral history given his unreliable self-report.
  8. Note that withdrawal monitoring is also needed.

Common presentation problems

Repair the habits that make a case hard to follow

Waiting for a confirmed thiamine level before treating

Repair: State that this is a clinical diagnosis treated empirically without delay.

Giving glucose first without considering thiamine timing

Repair: State that thiamine is given before or with glucose whenever feasible.

Withholding glucose entirely out of caution

Repair: State that clinically significant hypoglycemia is still treated immediately regardless of thiamine timing.

Treating confabulation as unrelated confusion rather than a specific finding

Repair: Name it explicitly as part of the encephalopathy and a reason to seek collateral history.

Frequently asked questions

Oral case presentation questions

Does he need all three triad features to make this diagnosis?

No, the full triad is present in only a minority of cases; clinical suspicion should remain high with even one or two features in a patient with a compatible nutritional and alcohol history, since under-recognition is the greater risk.

How is this different from simple alcohol intoxication?

Simple intoxication typically improves as alcohol clears over hours, while the ataxia, eye findings, and encephalopathy of Wernicke encephalopathy do not resolve with observation alone and specifically require thiamine treatment to prevent progression to irreversible Korsakoff syndrome.

How long should this presentation be?

This worked example targets about 80 seconds. A patient with airway compromise or severe withdrawal would need a more urgent, focused initial presentation.
