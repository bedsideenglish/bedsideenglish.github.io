Quick answer

How should I present a cauda equina syndrome case on rounds?

Lead with the red-flag triad—saddle anesthesia, bladder dysfunction, and bilateral leg weakness—since together they define a surgical emergency. Give the exam findings and bladder-scan volume, then the MRI findings before naming cauda equina syndrome. End with catheterization, the urgency of surgical decompression, and the exam details still needed.

Before you speak

Define the moment and the information you actually have

Setting

An emergency-department presentation after the bedside bladder scan and emergency MRI results return.

Audience

The on-call spine-surgery team deciding on the timing of decompressive surgery.

Available data

History, vital signs, a focused neurologic and rectal exam, a bladder scan, and an emergency MRI. Full lower-limb strength grading and sensory mapping are not itemized.

Target length

About 85 seconds

Fictional training case: The presentation is locked to the simulated patient JSON. It names important unknowns instead of inventing an examination, vital signs, allergies, or tests.

Complete spoken example

About 85 seconds practice script

Ms. Baker is a 45-year-old woman with chronic low back pain who now has bilateral leg weakness, saddle anesthesia, and urinary retention after heavy lifting.

She cannot feel toilet paper when wiping and has been unable to void for 14 hours despite feeling that her bladder is full. She is terrified of permanent paralysis and bladder incontinence.

She has a known L4-L5 herniated disc previously managed conservatively with physical therapy, and takes naproxen and cyclobenzaprine; she works as an accountant.

Her vitals are unremarkable at heart rate 88 and blood pressure 130 over 80. She has decreased perianal sensation, diminished Achilles reflexes, and lax anal sphincter tone on exam. Her bladder scan showed 650 milliliters of retained urine, and emergency MRI confirmed a large central L4-L5 disc extrusion compressing the cauda equina nerve roots.

My diagnosis is cauda equina syndrome from an acute central L4-L5 disc herniation, confirmed by the saddle anesthesia, urinary retention, lax sphincter tone, and MRI findings together.

I have placed a Foley catheter for bladder decompression and am obtaining immediate spine-surgery consultation for emergent decompressive surgery, ideally within 24 hours to maximize the chance of neurologic recovery.

Build the presentation

Give every sentence one job

A presentation is a compressed clinical argument, not a chart read aloud. These six moves keep the facts connected to your reasoning.

01

Opening one-liner

Name the red-flag triad immediately

“Ms. Baker is a 45-year-old woman with chronic low back pain who now has bilateral leg weakness, saddle anesthesia, and urinary retention after heavy lifting.”

Why it belongs: Saddle anesthesia, bladder dysfunction, and bilateral leg weakness together are the classic red-flag triad that separates cauda equina syndrome from routine back pain and demands emergency imaging.

Less clear

This is a 45-year-old with worse back pain than usual.

Prefer

Ms. Baker now has bilateral leg weakness, saddle anesthesia, and urinary retention after heavy lifting.

Why: Naming the specific red-flag triad in the opening sentence is what should prompt an emergency response.

02

History of present illness

Give the specific bladder and sensory findings

“She cannot feel toilet paper when wiping and has been unable to void for 14 hours despite feeling that her bladder is full. She is terrified of permanent paralysis and bladder incontinence.”

Why it belongs: Painless inability to void despite a full-bladder sensation, combined with saddle numbness, is more specific for cauda equina syndrome than back pain severity alone.

Less clear

She's having trouble with numbness and bladder problems.

Prefer

She cannot feel toilet paper when wiping and has been unable to void for 14 hours despite feeling her bladder is full.

Why: The specific sensory and bladder findings carry the diagnostic weight, not a vague description.

03

Relevant background

Connect this to her known disc disease

“She has a known L4-L5 herniated disc previously managed conservatively with physical therapy, and takes naproxen and cyclobenzaprine; she works as an accountant.”

Why it belongs: Her known disc disease at the same level explains the anatomic source of this acute deterioration and supports the working diagnosis before imaging confirms it.

Less clear

She has a history of a slipped disc.

Prefer

She has a known L4-L5 herniated disc previously managed conservatively, now with acute worsening after heavy lifting.

Why: Naming the specific level and prior management connects the history directly to today's emergency.

04

Objective data

State vitals, the exam, and the imaging findings

“Her vitals are unremarkable at heart rate 88 and blood pressure 130 over 80. She has decreased perianal sensation, diminished Achilles reflexes, and lax anal sphincter tone on exam. Her bladder scan showed 650 milliliters of retained urine, and emergency MRI confirmed a large central L4-L5 disc extrusion compressing the cauda equina nerve roots.”

Why it belongs: The lax sphincter tone and large post-void residual are objective confirmation of the bladder and sphincter dysfunction, and the MRI identifies the exact compressive lesion that surgery will address.

Less clear

Her bladder scan and MRI were both abnormal.

Prefer

Her bladder scan showed 650 milliliters of retained urine, and MRI confirmed a large central disc extrusion compressing the cauda equina.

Why: Exact volumes and the specific compressive finding tell the surgical team precisely what they are treating.

05

Assessment

Commit to the diagnosis as a surgical emergency

“My diagnosis is cauda equina syndrome from an acute central L4-L5 disc herniation, confirmed by the saddle anesthesia, urinary retention, lax sphincter tone, and MRI findings together.”

Why it belongs: The assessment names the diagnosis as a defined surgical emergency and ties it directly to the confirmed compressive lesion rather than restating the symptoms.

Less clear

This looks like a bad disc problem.

Prefer

My diagnosis is cauda equina syndrome from an acute central L4-L5 disc herniation.

Why: Naming the specific syndrome and level is what prompts emergency surgical referral rather than routine back-pain management.

06

Plan

State catheterization and the urgency of surgery

“I have placed a Foley catheter for bladder decompression and am obtaining immediate spine-surgery consultation for emergent decompressive surgery, ideally within 24 hours to maximize the chance of neurologic recovery.”

Why it belongs: Bladder decompression addresses an immediate complication, while stating the surgical timeframe conveys the urgency without promising a specific outcome.

Less clear

We'll catheterize her and get surgery involved at some point.

Prefer

I have placed a Foley catheter and am obtaining immediate spine-surgery consultation for decompression within 24 hours.

Why: Naming the specific timeframe reflects the true urgency of this diagnosis.

Do not fill the blanks

Say what has not yet been established

Real presentations often happen before the chart is complete. A brief, explicit limitation is safer and more credible than a polished invention.

Detailed lower-limb strength grading

Say: “A formal muscle-strength grading for each leg is not itemized in this simulation beyond 'bilateral weakness,' and I would document it precisely on exam.”

Exact strength grades help track improvement or deterioration after surgery.

Duration of symptoms before assessment

Say: “The precise time saddle anesthesia and retention began, beyond the 14-hour estimate, is not further specified, and confirming it helps counsel on the recovery window.”

Time from symptom onset to decompression influences prognosis and should be pinned down as precisely as possible.

After the presentation

Likely attending questions

Answer the question first, then give the evidence. If the source case does not contain the answer, say what you would verify.

Why is the timing of surgery so urgent?

Neurologic recovery, particularly of bladder and bowel function, is thought to be better the sooner the compression is relieved, so decompression is arranged as an emergency rather than an elective case, generally within 24 to 48 hours.

Why catheterize before surgery?

A large post-void residual with retention risks bladder overdistension injury, so a Foley catheter is placed immediately for decompression while surgical arrangements proceed.

What distinguishes this from a routine disc herniation?

The specific combination of saddle anesthesia, bladder or bowel dysfunction, and bilateral leg findings distinguishes cauda equina syndrome from a routine unilateral radiculopathy, which does not carry the same emergency urgency.

Self-check

A 30-second check before you present

  1. Name the red-flag triad in the opening sentence.
  2. Ask specifically about saddle sensation and bladder function.
  3. State the post-void residual volume, not just 'urinary retention.'
  4. Give the exact MRI finding and level.
  5. Confirm sphincter tone on rectal exam as an objective sign.
  6. State that a catheter has been placed for bladder decompression.
  7. State the urgency and target timeframe for surgery.
  8. Note what strength grading or timing details still need documentation.

Common presentation problems

Repair the habits that make a case hard to follow

Presenting this as routine worsening back pain

Repair: Name the red-flag triad explicitly to signal a surgical emergency.

Saying 'bladder problems' without a measured volume

Repair: State the post-void residual in milliliters from the bladder scan.

Delaying catheterization until after imaging

Repair: State that a catheter was placed immediately for bladder decompression.

Giving a vague surgical timeframe like 'soon'

Repair: State a specific target window, generally within 24 to 48 hours.

Frequently asked questions

Oral case presentation questions

Do I need to do a rectal exam if the patient is uncomfortable?

Yes, sphincter tone is a key objective finding in suspected cauda equina syndrome, and its absence or presence should be documented rather than skipped.

Should I mention conus medullaris syndrome as a differential?

Yes, briefly, since it is an important related diagnosis with overlapping findings; note that the presenting details here fit cauda equina syndrome, but say so explicitly rather than assuming the listener will infer it.

How long should this presentation be?

This worked example targets about 85 seconds. A rapidly worsening patient may need an even faster initial escalation focused on the red-flag triad alone.
