Before you walk in

The chart for today’s visit

Two-week review after starting amlodipine. This is what the doctor knows before the conversation starts.

Last visit

Two weeks ago: office BP 168/96 with intermittent morning headaches. No chest pain, dyspnea, focal weakness or visual loss. Amlodipine was started and home BP monitoring was requested.

Current medicines

  • Amlodipine 5 mg once daily
  • Metformin 1000 mg twice daily

Previous plan

  • Start amlodipine 5 mg daily
  • Record home BP twice daily
  • Reduce dietary sodium
  • Review in 2 weeks

New results and observations

  • Baseline creatinine 1.0 mg/dL
  • Potassium 4.2 mmol/L
  • No documented drug allergies

Today’s tasks

  • Assess BP response
  • Check adherence and side effects
  • Screen for red flags
  • Agree on the next treatment and monitoring plan

Model follow-up

Full transcript

  1. Doctor

    Mr. Kim, good to see you again. I'm Dr. Lee. It's been two weeks since we started amlodipine for your blood pressure. What would you like us to focus on today?

  2. Patient

    Two things. My ankles have been swelling, and I want to know if that's dangerous. And I want to know if I'll need another medicine.

  3. Doctor

    Good, we'll cover both. First, let me get a picture of how things are going. What have your home readings been?

  4. Patient

    Mostly around 145 to 155 on top, and 85 to 92 on the bottom.

  5. Doctor

    Thanks for keeping a record. That's better than the 168 over 96 we measured two weeks ago. How about the morning headaches?

  6. Patient

    They're less frequent. I still get one sometimes, but not like before.

  7. Doctor

    That's good progress. Many people miss a dose or two while they're building a new routine. How has taking it every day worked for you?

  8. Patient

    I missed two doses in the first week. I just wasn't used to it yet.

  9. Doctor

    That's very common. Is there something you do every morning without fail?

  10. Patient

    I always have breakfast before work.

  11. Doctor

    Then keeping the pill next to where you eat breakfast can make it automatic. Now, tell me about the ankle swelling.

  12. Patient

    Both ankles puff up a bit in the evening. By the morning, it's gone.

  13. Doctor

    That's a helpful description. Swelling in both ankles that builds up during the day and is gone by morning is a known side effect of amlodipine. The medicine relaxes small blood vessels, and a little fluid collects around the ankles. It isn't a sign of heart or kidney failure, but I'll still take a look at your ankles today. Is it bothering you much?

  14. Patient

    Not really. I just didn't know what it was.

  15. Doctor

    Have you had a severe headache, chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting, weakness, or a change in your vision?

  16. Patient

    No, none of those.

  17. Doctor

    Good. What concerns do you have about the medicine itself?

  18. Patient

    I have diabetes, and I already take metformin. I'm worried that taking several medicines will damage my kidneys.

  19. Doctor

    That's a fair question. Your kidney and potassium tests before we started were normal. Amlodipine doesn't need regular kidney blood tests. And bringing your blood pressure down is one of the best ways to protect your kidneys, especially with diabetes. If we add a second medicine later, some types need a quick blood test a week or two after starting. That's routine checking, not a sign of damage.

  20. Patient

    That makes me feel better. So, do I need another medicine?

  21. Doctor

    Your readings are better, but they're still above where we want them. I'll check your blood pressure here today as well. If it's still high, there are two main options: increase the amlodipine dose, or add a second medicine. A higher dose can make the ankle swelling a little more noticeable, so that's worth weighing up. What matters most to you?

  22. Patient

    I'd like to keep it simple. One pill a day, if possible.

  23. Doctor

    That's reasonable. We'll check your blood pressure now and choose together, looking for an option you can take once a day. Keep checking your blood pressure at home twice a day, keep cutting back on salt, and we'll set a date to review your readings.

  24. Patient

    Okay. That sounds manageable.

  25. Doctor

    If the swelling becomes painful, affects only one leg, or you get short of breath, call us. Call 911 if you get a sudden severe headache, chest pain, weakness, or a change in your vision. Can you tell me what you'll do between now and your next visit?

  26. Patient

    Take the pill every morning with breakfast, check my blood pressure twice a day, and cut back on salt. Call you if the swelling gets worse or it's only one leg. Call 911 for a bad headache, chest pain, weakness, or vision changes.

  27. Doctor

    Perfect. Let's check your blood pressure and your ankles now.

Flow at a glance

The route through the visit

  1. Set the agenda with the patient’s two questions
  2. Compare home readings and headaches with the first visit
  3. Normalize missed doses and link the pill to a habit
  4. Characterize the new side effect
  5. Screen for hypertensive emergency symptoms
  6. Answer the kidney worry with baseline results
  7. Offer options and agree on a simple plan

Do not miss

What changes the next step

  • Home readings improved but still above target: plan the next step
  • Severe headache, chest pain, breathlessness, weakness, or vision change
  • Swelling in one leg, painful swelling, or breathlessness: not a typical drug effect
  • The practical reason behind missed doses
  • An in-clinic blood pressure alongside the home log

Reusable language

Phrases to take into your next follow-up

“Many people miss a dose or two while they’re building a new routine.”
Makes it easy to admit missed doses.
“Is there something you do every morning without fail?”
Finds an existing habit to anchor the pill to.
“It isn’t a sign of heart or kidney failure, but I’ll still take a look at your ankles today.”
Reassures while still examining.
“That’s worth weighing up. What matters most to you?”
Hands the trade-off to the patient for a shared decision.
“Can you tell me what you’ll do between now and your next visit?”
Teach-back framed around actions, not facts.
